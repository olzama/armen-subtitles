Most of these are **winged film quotes**—lines Russians reuse in conversation—rather than internet memes. The English goal is to make them just as easy to quote while preserving the character’s voice, not to add modern meme slang.

| Passage | Why it sticks; what English needs |
|---|---|
| **«Кулинар!»** (“Chef!”) | A grand one-word address for an ordinary domestic moment. Keep it abrupt and mock-ceremonious; “cook” sounds too plain. |
| **«А кто не пьёт? Назови! … Я жду» / «Заметьте, не я это предложил!»** (“Who *doesn’t* drink? Name one. I’m waiting.” / “Mind you, it wasn’t my idea!”) | Velurov indignantly defends drinking, then eagerly accepts a drink while disowning the suggestion. Preserve the challenge, the pause, and the transparent excuse: the latter makes the exchange reusable. |
| **«Высокие, высокие отношения»** (“Such lofty, *lofty* relations.”) | Apparent praise for a civilized arrangement that also lets Margarita control her ex-husband. Keep the repetition and leave room for irony. |
| **«Хоботов, это мелко!»** (“Khobotov, that’s petty!”) | Margarita turns a small act of independence into a moral offence. *Мелко* means petty, not physically small; her reproach should sound commanding. |
| **«Я вся такая внезапная… такая противоречивая вся!»** (“I’m so unpredictable … such a bundle of contradictions!”) | Anna Adamovna makes indecision sound like an irresistible personality trait. This line also circulates as a meme template. Keep the theatrical self-description; “sudden” misses the joke. |
| **«Ну, юноша, самовыражайтесь быстрее»** (“Come on, young man—express yourself a little faster.”) | A lofty invitation to “express yourself” doubles as an impatient hurry-up. Preserve that collision of registers. |
| **«Искусство в большом долгу»** (“Art has a lot to answer for.”) | A sweeping, compact verdict on art’s failure to live up to life. An idiomatic English phrase carries more wit than “art is in great debt.” |
| **«Это мой крест! И нести его мне»** (“He’s my cross to bear!”) | Margarita presents caring for Khobotov as a sacrifice while refusing to let another woman have him. Keep the religious metaphor and self-dramatization. |
| **«Неблагодарный эпилептик!» / «Умалишённых не регистрируют!»** (“You ungrateful epileptic!” / “They don’t register marriages for lunatics!”) | Her insults expose the possessiveness beneath her supposed concern. Don’t soften them into kindly advice: the cruelty is part of her characterization. |
| **«Нет у меня никакого приступа!» — «Будет!» / «Резать к чёртовой матери…»** (“I’m not having an attack!”—“You will!” / “Cut the damn thing out—before peritonitis sets in!”) | The brisk prediction turns care into a threat; the famous surgical order makes drastic intervention sound absurdly obvious. Keep its forceful rhythm so it can also work figuratively. |
| **«На всех языках говоришь, а по-русски не понимаешь. Живут не для радости, а для совести»** (“You speak every language, yet you don’t understand Russian. We don’t live for pleasure; we live by our conscience.”) | Savva sincerely offers a moral lesson while endorsing the control Lev wants to escape. Keep **conscience**, not merely “duty,” and let the irony arise from the scene. |
| **«Партия переходит в эндшпиль… Савранский, ты дождался своего звёздного часа»** (“The game’s entering the endgame… Savransky, your moment of glory has come.”) | Kostik frames the rescue as a chess strategy, then grandly summons its unlikely hero. Retain the chess term and playful elevation. |
| **«Больные не выдадут… У больных большая взаимовыручка»** (“The patients won’t give us away… Patients stick together.”) | An ordinary hospital ward becomes a band of conspirators. “Patients” is the natural, non-disparaging choice. |
| **«Фарс! Водевиль! Трагикомедия! Смешение жанров…»** (“Farce! Vaudeville! Tragicomedy! A mash-up of genres!”) | Lev protests that the escape plan is theatrical; Kostik embraces precisely that. Preserve the escalating list and the film’s self-aware playfulness. |
| **«Под маской овцы таился лев» and the *Hamlet* recitation** | Velurov inflates a flimsy cover story into a performance. *Лев* is both “lion” and Khobotov’s name: “Beneath a sheep’s disguise lurked a lion—Lev” can carry both. Use recognizable English *Hamlet* wording where timing allows. |
| **«Поверьте историку: осчастливить против желания нельзя»** (“Take it from a historian: you can’t make someone happy against their will.”) | Kostik’s mock-authoritative credential delivers the film’s answer to coercive “care.” Keep **make someone happy**, rather than weakening it to “help.” |
| **«Догнать Савранского — это утопия!»** (“Catching Savransky? That’s a pipe dream!”) | A lofty-sounding final verdict on a rider who is already gone. “Pipe dream” is immediately idiomatic; “utopia” better preserves the speaker’s comic grandiosity. |

**Recognizable quotations and songs need a different approach.** Nekrasov’s **«Поэтом можешь ты не быть…»** should sound like an elevated civic quotation, even amid Velurov’s sillier act. Pushkin’s **«Ах, обмануть меня не трудно…»** is an existing literary quotation, not a film-coined joke. Okudzhava’s Moscow songs, the bittersweet **“Love is never free of sadness…”** lyric, the *Faust* serenade, and the French polka refrain should be translated as lyrics or allusions: preserve their place names, emotional contrasts, or comic incongruity rather than inventing punchlines.

=== CHUNK 1 ===
These subtitles are from **_The Pokrovsky Gate_** (*Покровские ворота*), Mikhail Kozakov’s 1982 film about residents of a 1950s Moscow communal apartment. Many of its lines became *крылатые фразы*—quotable catchphrases. That is a better description than calling every line an internet meme, though some also circulate as clips and meme templates. The times below refer to **your subtitles**. ([mosfilm.ru](https://www.mosfilm.ru/cinema/films/pokrovskie-vorota/?utm_source=openai))

| Time | Passage and suggested English | Why it matters to a translator |
|---|---|---|
| **06:12** | **«Кулинар!»** — “Chef!” | An unexpectedly grand, one-word address in an ordinary household scene. It is remembered as a film catchphrase; “Chef!” keeps the abrupt comic flourish better than “Cook!” ([aif.ru](https://aif.ru/society/history/kvartirnyy-vopros-pokrovskiy-vorot-mog-li-hobotov-postroit-kvartiru?ysclid=mpzvchdtjh537458170&utm_source=openai)) |
| **26:58–27:49** | **«А кто не пьёт? Назови! … Я жду»** — “Who *doesn’t* drink? Name one. I’m waiting.” **«А не хлопнуть ли нам по рюмашке?» — «Заметьте, не я это предложил!»** — “How about a quick drink?” “Mind you, it wasn’t my idea!” | Velurov indignantly defends his drinking, then eagerly accepts an invitation while disclaiming responsibility. The second exchange is particularly reusable as a joke about accepting something one secretly wants. Both the exchange and his delivery, not just the literal words, are the point. ([info.wikireading.ru](https://info.wikireading.ru/223846)) |
| **33:12–33:17; 43:23–43:33** | **«Высокие, высокие отношения»** — “Such lofty, *lofty* relations.” | Said of Margarita, her ex-husband Khobotov and her new partner Savva sharing an apparently civilized domestic arrangement. The praise is funny because the arrangement also leaves Khobotov subject to Margarita’s control. It can be used ironically of complicated relationships presented as admirably mature. Preserve the repetition. ([ls.pushkininstitute.ru](https://ls.pushkininstitute.ru/storage/kino/pokrvor_af_1/?utm_source=openai)) |
| **37:06–37:08**; also **11:43** | **«Хоботов, это мелко!»** — “Khobotov, that’s petty!” | Margarita treats Khobotov’s small act of independence as a moral failing. *Мелко* means petty or small-minded, **not** “small” in size; her commanding, reproachful tone is essential. ([info.wikireading.ru](https://info.wikireading.ru/223846)) |
| **48:59–49:10** | **«Поэтом можешь ты не быть, но гражданином быть обязан»** — “You needn’t be a poet, but you must be a citizen.” | This is **Nikolay Nekrasov’s famous line**, quoted by Velurov—not a saying coined by the film. Here it helps his stage act make an earnest, officially respectable claim to civic purpose after silly comic verses. An English subtitle should retain the elevated, familiar-quotation feel. ([ar.culture.ru](https://ar.culture.ru/ru/subject/kopii-s-podlinnyh-pisem-husena-svoemu-druguk?utm_source=openai)) |
| **1:11:46–1:11:58** | **«Я вся такая внезапная… такая противоречивая вся!»** — “I’m so unpredictable … such a bundle of contradictions!” | Anna Adamovna presents her indecision as a charmingly dramatic personality trait. This is one of the film’s best-known lines and has circulated as an actual meme template. “Sudden” is too literal for *внезапная* here; “unpredictable” carries the joke. ([culture.ru](https://www.culture.ru/live/movies/2699/pokrovskie-vorota?utm_source=openai)) |
| **1:22:54–1:23:01** | **«Ну, юноша, самовыражайтесь быстрее»** — “Come on, young man—express yourself a little faster.” | Kostik briskly interrupts someone at chess. The clash between the lofty *самовыражайтесь* (“express yourself”) and impatience makes it useful as a playful hurry-up line. ([lifehacker.ru](https://lifehacker.ru/krylatye-frazy-iz-sovetskih-filmov/amp/?utm_source=openai)) |
| **1:20:57–1:21:01**; also **35:59–36:05** | **«Искусство в большом долгу»** — “Art has a lot to answer for.” | A compact, quotable cultural verdict: art has fallen short of what life demands of it. “Art is in great debt” reproduces the words but misses the idiomatic wit. ([info.wikireading.ru](https://info.wikireading.ru/223846)) |
| **1:38:46–1:38:55** | **«Это мой крест! И нести его мне»** — “He’s my cross to bear!” | Margarita calls caring for Khobotov a sacrifice **while arguing that no other woman should take him away**. The religious metaphor and self-dramatization are important; it is a well-known quotation from the film. ([culture.ru](https://www.culture.ru/live/movies/2699/pokrovskie-vorota?utm_source=openai)) |

**Songs and quotations need a different label from memes.** The opening **«Живописцы, окуните ваши кисти…»** (00:50–02:32) and **«Ах, Арбат, мой Арбат…»** (45:46–47:50) are recognizable songs by **Bulat Okudzhava**, not dialogue invented as film punchlines. His “Song about the Arbat” calls the Moscow street the singer’s “calling,” “religion,” and “homeland”: those escalating words make a street into a place of belonging. **«Часовые любви»** (1:17:41–1:19:07) is another Okudzhava song used in the film; its “sentries of love” extend the Moscow-and-love motif. Translate them as lyrics, retaining the place names and emotional progression rather than seeking joke equivalents. ([music.mosfilm.ru](https://music.mosfilm.ru/news/novost-detaln/?ELEMENT_ID=61078&utm_source=openai))

Likewise, **«Ах, обмануть меня не трудно. Я сам обманываться рад»** (1:34:10–1:34:15)—“It’s easy to deceive me; I’m happy to deceive myself”—is **Pushkin**, recited in the film. Its cultural force predates the movie. ([culture.ru](https://www.culture.ru/poems/4451/priznanie?utm_source=openai))

**Scope caution:** online lists often lead with **«Резать к чёртовой матери, не дожидаясь перитонитов!»** (“Cut it out, damn it—don’t wait for peritonitis!”). It is indeed a famous line from *this film*, but it **does not occur in the subtitles supplied**, so it should not be assigned a timestamp here. ([kino.rambler.ru](https://kino.rambler.ru/movies/53573398-zamette-ne-ya-eto-predlozhil-nazvany-samye-populyarnye-frazy-iz-pokrovskih-vorot/?utm_source=openai))

=== CHUNK 2 ===
These subtitles are from *Pokrovsky Gates* (*Покровские ворота*), a film made in 1982 and first shown on television in 1983. Several lines here are **winged film quotes**—phrases Russians reuse in conversation—rather than internet memes with a single image or template. The distinction matters: a translator should preserve the character’s voice and the joke, not make every memorable line sound like modern meme slang. ([culture.ru](https://www.culture.ru/materials/56476/oleg-menshikov-citaty-iz-filma-pokrovskie-vorota))

| Time | Passage and status | What an English translator should know |
|---|---|---|
| **1:43:13** | **«Неблагодарный эпилептик!» / «Умалишённых не регистрируют!»** Famous character lines. ([ru.wikiquote.org](https://ru.wikiquote.org/wiki/%D0%9F%D0%BE%D0%BA%D1%80%D0%BE%D0%B2%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B8%D0%B5_%D0%B2%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%BE%D1%82%D0%B0_%28%D1%84%D0%B8%D0%BB%D1%8C%D0%BC%29)) | Margarita answers Lev’s declaration that he will marry by insulting him and claiming that the mentally ill cannot register a marriage. The comedy is **her possessive certainty that she may decide his life for him**; it is not an invitation to soften her cruelty. “You ungrateful epileptic!” and “They don’t register marriages for lunatics!” convey the attack, though the latter is deliberately harsh and period-flavoured. |
| **1:43:23–1:44:05** | **«Нет у меня никакого приступа!» — «Будет!»; «Резать к чёртовой матери. Не дожидаясь перитонитов».** The surgical order is among the film’s best-known quotations; a study of social-media uses ranked it first among the film’s phrases for the period it measured. ([ru.wikiquote.org](https://ru.wikiquote.org/wiki/%D0%9F%D0%BE%D0%BA%D1%80%D0%BE%D0%B2%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B8%D0%B5_%D0%B2%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%BE%D1%82%D0%B0_%28%D1%84%D0%B8%D0%BB%D1%8C%D0%BC%29)) | “I’m not having an attack!”—“You will!” exposes the threat beneath the supposed concern. The surgeon’s brisk “Cut the damn thing out—before peritonitis sets in!” is funny because everyone treats an unwanted operation as an enlightened, obvious solution. **«Перитонитов» is plural in the famous film line**; the singular often appears in retellings. The quote also gets applied figuratively to drastic action. ([bolshoyvopros.ru](https://www.bolshoyvopros.ru/questions/2276148-rezat-k-chjortovoj-materi-ne-dozhidajas-peritonita-otkuda-fraza.html?utm_source=openai)) |
| **1:46:56–1:47:29** | **«Любовь никогда не бывает без грусти. Но это приятней, чем грусть без любви».** A culturally resonant song lyric, **not a line coined by this film**: the song also belongs to the earlier film *Hearts of Four*. ([ru.sberopedia.org](https://ru.sberopedia.org/wiki/%D0%A1%D0%B5%D1%80%D0%B4%D1%86%D0%B0_%D1%87%D0%B5%D1%82%D1%8B%D1%80%D1%91%D1%85_%28%D1%84%D0%B8%D0%BB%D1%8C%D0%BC%29?utm_source=openai)) | “Love is never free of sadness—but that’s better than sadness without love.” Keep the simple, bittersweet contrast. Unlike the surgical line, this is a sincere nostalgic musical moment, not a punchline. |
| **1:50:52** | **«Мой совет: до обрученья ты не целуй его!»** An older musical quotation, not a film-origin meme. ([conservatory.ru](https://www.conservatory.ru/sites/default/files/uploads/rio/academic%20publictions/%D0%A4%D0%B0%D1%83%D1%81%D1%82_%D0%91%D1%83%D0%BA%D0%BB%D0%B5%D1%82_1948.pdf?utm_source=openai)) | This comes from the Russian text of Mephistopheles’ serenade in Gounod’s *Faust*. “My advice: don’t kiss him before you’re engaged!” works in subtitles; its priggish advice is comically incongruous coming from Mephistopheles and in this flirtatious scene. |
| **1:54:12–1:54:51** | **«Мон папа, не же па…»** A playful song and plot motif, rather than an independently famous meme. ([teksti-pesen.com](https://teksti-pesen.com/lyrics/15/Pokrovskie-vorota/tekst-pesni-Polechka?utm_source=openai)) | The subtitle is a **phonetic Russian rendering of French**, imperfectly transcribed here: *Mon papa ne veut pas que je danse la polka*—“My father doesn’t want me to dance the polka.” The family’s prohibition makes the dancing a small, cheerful act of defiance. Retain that connection rather than treating the sounds as nonsense. |
| **1:58:49–1:59:01** | **«На всех языках говоришь, а по-русски не понимаешь. Живут не для радости, а для совести».** A widely quoted line. ([kinomania.net](https://www.kinomania.net/movie/695774/quotes?utm_source=openai)) | Savva tells multilingual Lev that he fails to understand the supposedly elementary moral rule: “We don’t live for pleasure; we live by our conscience.” **«Совесть» is “conscience,”** not simply “duty.” The irony is essential: Savva sincerely thinks he is helping while endorsing the arrangement Lev is desperate to escape. |
| **2:00:01–2:00:15** | **«Партия переходит в эндшпиль… Совранский, ты дождался своего звёздного часа».** A quotable setup to the rescue. ([citaty.ru](https://citaty.ru/c/iz-filmov/pokrovskie-vorota/?utm_source=openai)) | Kostik casts the crisis as a chess game: “We’re into the endgame, ladies—and I’m playing it.” Savransky’s “moment of glory” is to ride Lev away. **His name is normally spelled Савранский (Savransky),** though these subtitles repeatedly give Совранский. ([ru.wikiquote.org](https://ru.wikiquote.org/wiki/%D0%9F%D0%BE%D0%BA%D1%80%D0%BE%D0%B2%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B8%D0%B5_%D0%B2%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%BE%D1%82%D0%B0_%28%D1%84%D0%B8%D0%BB%D1%8C%D0%BC%29)) |
| **2:00:42–2:00:46** | **«Больные не выдадут. … У больных большая взаимовыручка».** A smaller but recognized film exchange. ([ru.wikiquote.org](https://ru.wikiquote.org/wiki/%D0%9F%D0%BE%D0%BA%D1%80%D0%BE%D0%B2%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B8%D0%B5_%D0%B2%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%BE%D1%82%D0%B0_%28%D1%84%D0%B8%D0%BB%D1%8C%D0%BC%29)) | “The patients won’t give us away.”—“True. Patients stick together.” The joke is the conspiratorial solidarity attributed to an ordinary hospital ward. Here **больные** means *patients*, not “sick people” in a disparaging sense. |
| **2:00:51–2:01:01** | **«Фарс! Водевиль! Трагикомедия! Смешение жанров…»** A self-aware comic passage, less established as a standalone catchphrase. ([vothouse.ru](https://www.vothouse.ru/films/20100802/pokrovskiye_vorota_text.html)) | Lev calls the clothes-swap plan an absurd farce; Kostik enthusiastically embraces the description. “Farce! Vaudeville! Tragicomedy! A mash-up of genres!” preserves the escalation. It also describes the film’s own abrupt shifts between peril, performance and romance. |
| **2:02:58–2:03:51** | **«Под маской овцы таился лев» and the *Hamlet* recitation.** A celebrated comic performance, not an original *Hamlet* meme. ([ru.wikiquote.org](https://ru.wikiquote.org/wiki/%D0%9F%D0%BE%D0%BA%D1%80%D0%BE%D0%B2%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B8%D0%B5_%D0%B2%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%BE%D1%82%D0%B0_%28%D1%84%D0%B8%D0%BB%D1%8C%D0%BC%29)) | Velurov invents a melodramatic account of being stripped of his clothes, then suddenly quotes *Hamlet*. **«Лев» means “lion” and is also Lev Khobotov’s first name**: “Beneath a sheep’s disguise lurked a lion—Lev” can recover both senses. For the Shakespeare lines, use recognizable English *Hamlet* wording where timing permits; the joke depends on the grand theatrical register invading a flimsy cover story. |
| **2:04:59–2:05:10** | **«Поверьте историку: осчастливить против желания нельзя».** One of the film’s central, widely quoted statements. ([penrussia.org](https://www.penrussia.org/new/2014/2242?utm_source=openai)) | Kostik’s reply to Margarita sums up the conflict: **“Take it from a historian: you can’t make someone happy against their will.”** *Осчастливить* means *make someone happy*, not merely *help* them. His “historian” credential gives the youthful objection a mock-authoritative flourish. |
| **2:05:34–2:05:39** | **«Догнать Савранского — это утопия!»** A famous closing catchphrase, still quoted in contexts involving someone too fast to catch. ([ru.wikiquote.org](https://ru.wikiquote.org/wiki/%D0%9F%D0%BE%D0%BA%D1%80%D0%BE%D0%B2%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B8%D0%B5_%D0%B2%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%BE%D1%82%D0%B0_%28%D1%84%D0%B8%D0%BB%D1%8C%D0%BC%29)) | Savransky has sped away with Lev. “Catching Savransky? That’s a pipe dream!” is more idiomatic than a literal “That is a utopia,” although *utopia* preserves the character’s comically lofty word choice. |

**Translation priority:** the film’s most reusable lines work because they set **earnest moral certainty against another person’s freedom**. Margarita and Savva speak as if control were care; Kostik’s “you can’t make someone happy against their will” answers them. Keep that contrast audible even when shortening subtitles for reading speed. ([diletant.media](https://diletant.media/articles/38198838/?utm_source=openai))

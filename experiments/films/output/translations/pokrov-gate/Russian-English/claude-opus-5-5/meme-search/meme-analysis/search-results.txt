=== CHUNK 1 ===
This is **«Покровские ворота»** (Pokrovsky Gates, Mikhail Kozakov, 1982, from Leonid Zorin's play). The story is set in autumn 1956 to spring 1957, during the Thaw, in a Moscow communal flat. Many of its lines are standard Russian catchphrases. My web searches did little to confirm how widely each line is used today. The lines I could check against sources are marked **(src)**. The rest comes from my own knowledge of the film, so treat the order as a judgement call, not something I measured.

## Tier 1: well-known catchphrases (keep the wording exact and consistent)

| # | Line | Notes for the translator |
|---|---|---|
| 427, 581–582 | **«Высокие… высокие отношения»** / «Нормальные для духовных людей» | Possibly the film's most famous line. A guest says it, deadpan, about the ex-husband, the new husband and the wife all eating at one table. It's now a stock ironic comment on any odd love or ex-partner arrangement. The joke is the polite, high-minded register. Try something like *"Such… such elevated relations."* Use the same wording at 581 so the callback works. |
| 134, 488 | **«Хоботов, это мелко!»** | Margarita's put-down. Use one fixed rendering both times, e.g. *"Khobotov, that's petty!"* |
| 369–371 | «А не хлопнуть ли нам по рюмашке?» → **«Заметьте, не я это предложил»** | **(src: Kostik's line)** People quote this when accepting a drink while pretending it wasn't their idea. *"Mind you, I wasn't the one who suggested it."* Velyurov has a drinking problem (360–367), which is where the comedy comes from. |
| 898–899, 950–951 | **«Я вся такая внезапная, такая противоречивая вся»** | Anna Adamovna's self-dramatising line, now quoted to mock capricious, posing behaviour. The odd grammar (the trailing «вся») is the joke, so don't smooth it out: *"I'm so sudden… so contradictory, all of me."* Note that 898 says «несуразная/угловатая» and 950 says «внезапная». |
| 63, 68, 419, 800, 1025… | Savva's German: «Зер гут», **«Натюрлих»**, «Яволь», «Фюнф минут», «Айн момент», «Ахтунг», «хунд де грабен» | **(src: «Натюрлих, Маргарита Пална!» is a known meme)** It's soldier's German picked up in the war. Leave it in German, since English viewers will get it. «хунд де грабен» is a mangled *da liegt der Hund begraben*, and Savva glosses it himself in 570. «Ауфвидеркукен» (579) is a deliberate mangling. |
| 1009 ff. | Kostik's breakup excuses: Lake Titicaca city, **«череп коня Вещего Олега»**, Noah's Ark on Ararat, Tamerlane's grave, then **«Вычеркиваю»** | A running gag. The horse skull is from Pushkin's *Song of the Prophetic Oleg*, where the prince dies from a snake hidden in his horse's skull. English viewers won't know it, so consider *"Prince Oleg's horse's skull, the one with the snake"*. «Вычеркиваю» = *"Crossing you off."* |
| 380–452, 677–681, 1205, 1239 | The **dead-poets gag**: «Он что, тоже умер?», «Они все как сговорились», «Упал и умер», «Он плохо кончил», «Эмиль Золя угорел» | Camões, Villon and Rimbaud are real, and the facts given are roughly right. "Bertrand Dufois" seems to be invented. Griboyedov was killed in Tehran. Zola really did die of fumes from a blocked chimney, and «Выключайте конфорки» ("turn your burners off") mocks that. Keep the replies short and flat. |
| 1087–1088 | «Когда я на почте служил ямщиком, был молод, имел я **селёдку**» | A drunken garbling of the famous romance *Ямщик*, where the line ends «силёнку» (strength), turned into "herring". You need a sound-alike pun, e.g. *"…young, and I had my brawn… my prawn!"* |

## Tier 2: allusions that a literal translation would lose

- **826, 838: «Я тоскую, как Блок» / «какой-то Херасков».** Blok was a modern Silver Age poet. Kheraskov was a stuffy 18th-century classicist. The point is "you're not modern, you're antique", so something like *"more like some powdered-wig ode-writer"* works.
- **846–852: «мадам баттерфляй» → «Мотылёк!».** A pun on the butterfly swimming stroke. It works in English as it is.
- **1083: «по гамбургскому счёту».** Shklovsky's idiom for judging by the strictest professional standard. Render the meaning (*"no allowances, then?"*), because the literal "Hamburg score" means nothing in English.
- **1073: «решил переквалифицироваться».** An echo of Ostap Bender (*The Golden Calf*): «переквалифицироваться в управдомы». Hard to carry over; "retrain" is enough.
- **472, 1102: «Искусство в большом долгу».** A Soviet newspaper cliché, used ironically. Also 89–94, «поджигатели войны» / «Пой, ласточка, пой»: parody of Soviet peace propaganda. *"Warmongers"* is the standard English term.
- **104–108:** Yuri Levitan was the voice of Soviet radio.
- **638:** Nekrasov, «Поэтом можешь ты не быть…». Use a published translation.
- **1290–1303:** Pushkin, «Признание» ("Alina, have pity…").
- **1253–1255:** Lermontov, «Благодарность».
- **1273–1276:** Tyutchev / the romance «Я встретил вас».
- **1376–1377:** A. K. Tolstoy's epigram on Delarue ("stabbed… said politely: 'Thank you'"). It's the punchline to Margarita's «ещё скажешь мне спасибо» (1375), so the last word has to be "Thank you".
- **691: «Какой чёрт занёс вас на эти галеры?»** Molière (*Scapin*): *"What the devil was he doing in that galley?"* That English version exists and is quotable.
- **307: «Тартюф»** (Molière). **1284:** Matthew 7:1, which in the King James Version is *"Judge not, that ye be not judged."*
- **460–470: «Роза Муда».** **(src)** This is *Rosamunde*, the Czech polka known in English as the **Beer Barrel Polka** ("Roll Out the Barrel"). Savva sings it, not Khobotov. The Russian verses are a soldiers' demobilisation version, and the German lines are garbled forms of "Rosamunde, schenk mir dein Herz und sag ja…".
- **480, 1219–1224: «Mon papa ne veut pas…».** A French folk polka. Kostik's "translation" is deliberately a dull literal one, so keep it flat: *"My father forbade me to dance the polka."*
- **Margarita's French:** «пар де пи» = *par dépit* (out of spite), «ву зет ридикюль» = *vous êtes ridicule*, «се монифик» = *c'est magnifique*. Her pretentious French is set against Savva's soldier's German, and the subtitles should keep that contrast.

## Tier 3: quotable lines and wordplay

- **Double meanings in the injection scene (204–209):** «Вы прекрасно кололи», «Я ничего не почувствовал» / «Жаль». Margarita later jabs at him with «тебе надо… уколоться» (593), meaning both "get a shot" and "prick yourself". Keep the double sense.
- **506–507: «Раиса? — Людмила»** and **«Людочка, а не милочка».** «Милочка» is a condescending "dearie" that rhymes with Ludochka. *"Ludochka, not 'dearie'"* keeps it.
- **Margarita's pop-psychology:** «это кричит твой вакуум», «как все тайные эротоманы», «узкий лобик», «павиан», «Это мой крест».
- **Savva's lines:** «Характер такой, что фронтом командуй» **(src)**, «Он ломает, я чиню», «моё дело солдатское».
- **Velyurov:** «Я служу Мосэстраде», a pun on «служитель муз» ("servant of the Muses"). Mosestrada was the state variety-concert agency. Also «Я — Велюров!» and «Я попрошу без амикошонства!».
- **Kostik:** «Константин — в переводе с античного — постоянный», which is ironic for a womaniser. Also «Спит родимый аквариум» **(src)**, «Грядут перемены!» **(src)**, «вы — в ЗАГС, а Хоботов — в монастырь», «из общих ульев в личные гнёзда» (the move from communal flats to Khrushchev-era housing), «Независимые умы никогда не боялись банальностей».
- **Period markers:** the perfume «Красная Москва», the «Трудовые резервы» sports society, the 1957 World Youth Festival, and «Физкульт-привет!».

## Loose ends
- Who says «Высокие отношения» (Nina Andreevna or Gleb Nikolaevich Orlovich) is my recollection only; check it against the film.
- That the Delarue epigram is by A. K. Tolstoy is also from memory and wasn't verified.
- Possible subtitle mistakes: «идя» (1199) should be «идея»; «Камойнс» is Camões; «фаллехов гендекоссилаб / тактеля / цесуры» should be Phalaecian hendecasyllable / dactyl / caesura.
- I didn't identify the source of the "Как корабль…" poem (645–662).

=== CHUNK 2 ===
This is from **«Покровские ворота»** (*The Pokrovsky Gates*), the 1982 film directed by Mikhail Kozakov from Leonid Zorin's play. It's one of the most quoted Soviet comedies, and almost every line in this stretch has passed into everyday speech. Searches confirmed only some of what's below. Each item is marked ✔ (checked against a source) or ~ (from my own knowledge, not checked).

## Top-tier catchphrases (widely quoted on their own)

| Line | Context / why it's funny | Translator note |
|---|---|---|
| **«Резать к чёртовой матери, не дожидаясь перитонитов!»** ✔ | Surgeon Vera Semyonovna's brisk verdict on Khobotov's appendix. It's quoted as the motto for any drastic "just cut it off" fix. «Единственно прогрессивный взгляд» parodies Soviet ideological jargon. | Keep it blunt and a little crude ("Cut the damn thing out, no waiting for peritonitis!"). The plural «перитонитов» is comic in itself. |
| **«Я лежал на столе, обнажённый и беззащитный… Со мной могли сделать всё, что угодно!»** ✔ | Khobotov's melodramatic self-pity, punctured by the nurse's deadpan «Чего не надо, того не сделают». | Keep the high register so the nurse's flat reply lands. |
| **«Больные увидят. — Больные не выдадут.»** ✔ | The escape plot. The follow-up «у больных большая взаимовыручка» sounds like a Soviet slogan about mutual aid. | Officialese is the joke ("patients stand by one another"). |
| **«Живут не для радости, а для совести.»** ✔ | Savva's folk wisdom, quoted half-seriously as a life motto. | Make it an aphorism: "We live for conscience, not for joy." |
| **«История, леденящая кровь! Под маской овцы таился лев!»** ✔ | Velyurov's account of being "robbed" of his clothes. It flips "wolf in sheep's clothing", and *лев* is also Khobotov's name (Лев). | The Lev/lion pun is lost in English. Use "Leo" or a footnote, or accept the loss. |
| **«Умалишённых не регистрируют!»** ~ | Margarita: the registry office (ЗАГС) won't marry the insane. | "They don't marry off lunatics!" |
| **«Неблагодарный эпилептик!»** ~ | Comes right after Khobotov says he could have been *Dostoevsky*, who was famously epileptic. | Worth keeping both words so the echo survives. |
| **«Осчастливить против желания нельзя.»** ~ | Khobotov's dignified moral and the thesis of the whole film. | Aphoristic: "You can't make someone happy against their will." |
| **«На всех языках говоришь, а по-русски не понимаешь.»** ~ | Savva to the polyglot translator Khobotov. Proverbial. | Literal works. |

## Wordplay and cultural references

- **«Шевелюров… менее мануфактурно»** ~: The name Велюров sounds like *велюр* (velour, a fabric), so it's "textile-sounding". Шевелюров adds *шевелюра* ("a full head of hair"). «Грассируете» means rolling a French-style R, a mark of chic among old variety-stage singers. Neither pun survives literally. Consider an invented English pun, or explain that "Velour-ov" sounds like a fabric.
- **«Пора тебя брать!» / «Что значит брать?»** ~: *Брать* means "take (home)", but it also evokes "arrest" in the NKVD sense. That's why Khobotov panics. An English option is "time to take you in".
- **«Гемахт»** and **«Полный аллес капут»** ~: Savva's mangled wartime German (*gemacht*, *alles kaputt*). Keep the German. It's a character marker.
- **«Кого вы разбили под Аустерлицом?»** ~: Napoleon's victory at Austerlitz, as a joke on "you look like a conqueror". Works as is.
- **«Вы лицедей или банщик?» / «Где тяга актёра к переодеванию?»** ~: Mock theatrical rhetoric. «Фарс! Водевиль! Трагикомедия! Смешение жанров» is also the film's comment on itself.
- **«Или вы снова не в состоянии?» / «Опять?»** ~: These refer back to Velyurov's drunken episode earlier in the film. Keep the references consistent with how you translated those scenes.
- **«Совранский… не бросит стального друга»** ~: The "steel friend" is his motorcycle, in heroic-Soviet diction. «Догнать Совранского — это утопия!» is another quotable line.
- **«Ни пуха ни пера — К чёрту»** ~: The standard Russian good-luck ritual. Use "Break a leg" / "Thanks", or keep the ritual exchange.

## Embedded songs and quotations

- **Velyurov's couplets** («За гуманизм и дело мира бесстрашно борется сатира») ✔: A parody of Soviet stage satire in the style of Ilya Nabatov. Translate it as bad rhyming doggerel.
- **«Всё стало вокруг голубым и зелёным»** ✔: A song from the 1941 film *Сердца четырёх*. Velyurov sings it to Svetlana instead of his planned couplets. I couldn't confirm the songwriters.
- **«Тебя просил я быть на свидании… я у аптеки, а я в кино»** ~: A pre-war hit song, possibly a foxtrot. I couldn't confirm its origin. It's nostalgic, and the gag is that the lovers missed each other: one waited at the pharmacy, the other at the cinema.
- **«Мон папа, не же па…»** ~: A garbled transliteration of the French children's song *"Mon papa ne veut pas que je danse la polka"*. Use the real French in the English subtitles. Wikipedia notes this polka was cut from some versions of the film ✔.
- **«Мой совет: до обрученья ты не целуй его»** ~: Mephistopheles' serenade from Gounod's *Faust*, in a Russian version. Use the standard English libretto line or a close paraphrase.
- **«О, если бы моя тугая плоть могла растаять…»** ~: This is Hamlet's "O that this too too solid flesh would melt". I couldn't identify which Russian translation Velyurov is using; it may be a pastiche. Use Shakespeare's original. Kostik's «Тише, это Гамлет» only works if the audience recognizes the line.
- **«Олень подстреленный хрипит, лань уцелев резвится…»** ~: *Hamlet* III.2, "Why, let the strucken deer go weep, / The hart ungallèd play…". Again, use Shakespeare.
- **«По жизненной дороге с усилием передвигаю ноги» / «Не ем, не пью…»** ~: Khobotov's translations of a "French poetess". These are invented in-film parody, so translate them as deliberately overwrought verse.
- **Finale** («Москва молодеет, а я старею… Молодость, ты была или не была?… Нескучный сад») ✔: The grown-up Kostik's nostalgic voice-over and the film's emotional signature. Нескучный сад is a real Moscow park; keep the name, perhaps glossed.

## Loose ends
- I couldn't confirm who wrote the music and lyrics for «Всё стало вокруг голубым и зелёным» or «У аптеки».
- I couldn't identify the Russian *Hamlet* translation behind «тугая плоть» or «Олень подстреленный».
- The polka transliteration in the subtitles is garbled; check it against the French original before you rely on it as source text.

=== CHUNK 1 ===
These subtitles are from **_Gentlemen of Fortune_** (*Джентльмены удачи*, 1971). Many lines are better described as **winged film quotations**—phrases Russians repeat in conversation—than as memes that began online. The central translation challenge is that a gentle kindergarten director is impersonating a feared criminal: his tough talk keeps colliding with his manners and teacherly habits. ([dslov.ru](https://dslov.ru/ist/ist1.htm))

The most useful passages to flag are:

| Subtitle / time | Why it matters; English translation note |
|---|---|
| **3–4; 92–95; recurring — «Пасть порву! … Моргалы выколю!»** | One of the film’s signature threats: “I’ll tear your mouth off! I’ll gouge your eyes out!” *Пасть* (“maw”) and *моргалы* (rough slang for “eyes”) make the performance sound aggressively low-register. It is funnier because Troshkin repeatedly falls back on his few memorized threats. Don’t make him sound effortlessly fluent in criminal slang. ([dslov.ru](https://dslov.ru/ist/ist1.htm)) |
| **36 — «…пошью костюм с отливом и в Ялту!»** | Kosoy’s fantasy of spending the proceeds on a car with a tape recorder, a shiny suit, and a trip to the resort town of Yalta. It is a recognizable, quotable sketch of what riches look like to him. Keep the concrete, period-specific wish list rather than substituting modern luxuries. ([dvfu.ru](https://www.dvfu.ru/schools/school_of_humanities/humanities-research-in-the-russian-far-east/%D0%93%D1%83%D0%BC%D0%B0%D0%BD%D0%B8%D1%82%D0%B0%D1%80%D0%BD%D1%8B%D0%B5%20%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%81%D0%BB%D0%B5%D0%B4%D0%BE%D0%B2%D0%B0%D0%BD%D0%B8%D1%8F%20%E2%84%962%202018-compressed.pdf?utm_source=openai)) |
| **43 — «Всё! Кина не будет. Электричество кончилось.»** | A famous mock announcement—roughly, “That’s it, no movie. The power’s gone.” Kosoy says it as the police operation ends his plans. *Кина* is deliberately colloquial, not neutral *кино*; the line can jokingly announce that some anticipated event is over. ([dslov.ru](https://dslov.ru/pos/p1709.htm?utm_source=openai)) |
| **64–69 — «Так это в Турции, там тепло.»** | After a gruesome proposal for punishing thieves, Troshkin objects not to the cruelty but to the weather: “But that’s Turkey—it’s warm there.” Preserve the startlingly practical non sequitur. It is a memorable joke, though less of an independent catchphrase than the lines above. ([ru.wikiquote.org](https://ru.wikiquote.org/wiki/%D0%94%D0%B6%D0%B5%D0%BD%D1%82%D0%BB%D1%8C%D0%BC%D0%B5%D0%BD%D1%8B_%D1%83%D0%B4%D0%B0%D1%87%D0%B8?utm_source=openai)) |
| **70–75; 239–243 — «Нехороший человек? — Редиска.»** | Perhaps the key language joke. A purported criminal insult becomes *редиска*, literally “radish”; later Troshkin makes the thieves write down their slang and restate it in polite language. “Bad person? A radish” preserves the oddity; translating it simply as “jerk” loses the joke. A linguistic study cautions that the film’s definition should not be treated as a straightforward dictionary account of pre-film thieves’ argot—the film may itself have helped spread it. ([nm1925.ru](https://nm1925.ru/articles/2015/201506/pochemu-rediska-1585/)) |
| **75 — «Фрей-фея, действительно: фрей-фея.»** | Troshkin forgets the alleged slang for a *good* person and has to consult his notes. The obscure, amusingly hard-to-recall answer is part of the gag. A subtitle might retain “frey-feya” if the exchange can be followed visually, rather than inventing a transparent English underworld term. ([ru.wikiquote.org](https://ru.wikiquote.org/wiki/%D0%94%D0%B6%D0%B5%D0%BD%D1%82%D0%BB%D1%8C%D0%BC%D0%B5%D0%BD%D1%8B_%D1%83%D0%B4%D0%B0%D1%87%D0%B8)) |
| **93–95 — «…сарделька, сосиска, редиска, Навуходоносор, петух гамбургский!»** | Troshkin’s intimidation dissolves into a ludicrous inventory: foods, the biblical Nebuchadnezzar, and a “Hamburg rooster.” The accumulation matters more than decoding each item. An English version should sound increasingly improvised, not like a coherent string of serious insults. ([ru.wikiquote.org](https://ru.wikiquote.org/wiki/%D0%94%D0%B6%D0%B5%D0%BD%D1%82%D0%BB%D1%8C%D0%BC%D0%B5%D0%BD%D1%8B_%D1%83%D0%B4%D0%B0%D1%87%D0%B8)) |
| **112–114 — «Тут помню, тут… ничего.»** | Troshkin’s cover story is selective amnesia, conveniently affecting the whereabouts of the stolen helmet. It is commonly recalled in the tidier form **«Тут помню — тут не помню»** (“I remember this bit—but not that bit”). The subtitles give his actual hesitant wording, while another character says the more familiar formulation. Distinguish the on-screen line from its later catchphrase form. ([dslov.ru](https://dslov.ru/ist/ist1.htm)) |
| **162 — «Все побежали и я побежал.»** | Vasily’s disarmingly simple explanation for joining the escape: “Everyone ran, so I ran too.” Now a handy line for following a crowd without much thought. Its guilelessness is the point. ([dslov.ru](https://dslov.ru/ist/ist1.htm)) |
| **185 — «А в тюрьме сейчас ужин… макароны.»** | The hungry escapees are reminded that prison would, at least, be serving dinner: “And back in prison they’re having dinner now… pasta.” The joke depends on the bleak reversal—prison suddenly sounds appealing. ([dslov.ru](https://dslov.ru/ist/ist1.htm)) |
| **212; 216 — «Лошадью ходи, лошадью ходи, дурак!»** | An especially recognizable chess-scene quotation: “Move the knight! The knight, you idiot!” *Лошадь* is the everyday “horse,” not the proper Russian chess term *конь*. “Use the horse!” could preserve the character’s voice, provided the chessboard is visible; “Move your knight!” is clearer but smoother. ([info.wikireading.ru](https://info.wikireading.ru/223785?utm_source=openai)) |
| **230 — «Не играть, не пить, не воровать без меня.»** | “No gambling, no drinking, no stealing **without me**.” Troshkin seems to impose moral discipline, then adds a qualification that lets him keep posing as the gang leader. Put the punch line at the end in English. ([ru.wikiquote.org](https://ru.wikiquote.org/wiki/%D0%94%D0%B6%D0%B5%D0%BD%D1%82%D0%BB%D1%8C%D0%BC%D0%B5%D0%BD%D1%8B_%D1%83%D0%B4%D0%B0%D1%87%D0%B8)) |
| **256 — «…как три тополя на Плющихе.»** | “Like three poplars on Plyushchikha” means they are conspicuously standing around as a trio. It also names the well-known Soviet film *Three Poplars at Plyushchikha*—an existing cultural reference, **not** a phrase invented by *Gentlemen of Fortune*. A literal translation plus a brief note preserves both layers. ([premier.one](https://premier.one/show/tri-topolya-na-plyuschihe-tsvetnaya-versiya/facts?utm_source=openai)) |
| **292–293 — «Кто же его посадит? Он же памятник!»** | A major catchphrase. Asked whether the statue is *sitting*, Kosoy hears *посадить* as “put [someone] in prison”: “Who’d put him away? He’s a monument!” English cannot reproduce the sit/imprison overlap neatly; a subtitle may need to favor the immediate absurdity, with a translator’s note explaining the pun. ([dslov.ru](https://dslov.ru/ist/ist1.htm)) |
| **307 — «Кушать подано! Садитесь жрать, пожалуйста.»** | “Dinner is served! Sit down and **gobble**, please.” The collision of polite hospitality—*кушать*, *пожалуйста*—with coarse *жрать* is the whole joke. Neither an entirely genteel nor an entirely rude translation works. ([dslov.ru](https://dslov.ru/ist/ist1.htm)) |
| **324–326 — «Ты туда не ходи, ты сюда ходи… снег башка попадёт…»** | Vasily’s oft-quoted warning to pedestrians while the gang is ostensibly clearing snow: “Don’t go that way; go this way—snow hit head, you be completely dead!” His conspicuously nonstandard Russian and earnest, overdramatic warning give the line its sound. Suggest the broken syntax without turning him into a generic or demeaning ethnic caricature. ([dslov.ru](https://dslov.ru/ist/ist1.htm)) |
| **330 — «Вежливость — лучшее оружие вора.»** | “Politeness is a thief’s best weapon.” Troshkin turns courtesy into a tactic for getting past someone who might recognize him. This is a compact, independently quotable aphorism; preserve its mock-serious form. ([dslov.ru](https://dslov.ru/ist/ist1.htm)) |
| **383–384 — «Украл, выпил — в тюрьму… Романтика!»** | One of the film’s most famous lines: “Steal, drink, go to prison. Steal, drink, go to prison. How romantic!” Troshkin is **ironically** puncturing Kosoy’s glamorous self-image, not celebrating a criminal lifestyle. The deadpan final “Romance!” or “How romantic!” is essential. ([dslov.ru](https://dslov.ru/pos/6/p6_324.htm?utm_source=openai)) |
| **410–411 — «Ес, ес, ОБХС.»** | During a comically inadequate English lesson, “Yes, yes” turns into the sound of **ОБХСС**, the Soviet agency associated with investigating theft of socialist property and speculation. The subtitles’ “ОБХС” reflects the spoken gag; an English viewer will not recognize the initials without a note. Treat it as a sound-based Soviet institutional joke, not English vocabulary. ([info.wikireading.ru](https://info.wikireading.ru/223785?utm_source=openai)) |
| **554–555; 557 — «Чем больше сдадим — тем лучше» / «Сдаёмсу-у…»** | At the end, “The more we turn in, the better” leads to the gang’s elongated “We surrender!” *Сдать* can cover handing something or someone over; the payoff is that the former thieves are now delivering both the treasure and themselves to the police. Preserve the delayed realization and the drawn-out surrender. ([info.wikireading.ru](https://info.wikireading.ru/223785?utm_source=openai)) |

**Two cautions:** the subtitles also contain ordinary plot dialogue and several funny moments that are not equally established catchphrases; a quotation dictionary specifically records many of the lines above. And **«Проснись и пой»** at 01:12 is a culturally significant *song heard in the film*, not a line coined by its characters: it was written for a stage production and gained wider exposure through the film. ([info.wikireading.ru](https://info.wikireading.ru/223785?utm_source=openai))

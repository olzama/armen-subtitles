## Meme translation guide: *Gentlemen of Fortune*

These are mostly **winged film quotations**—lines reused in everyday Russian—rather than memes that originated online. The recurring comic engine is that Troshkin, a gentle kindergarten director posing as a hardened criminal, has only a thin repertoire of tough talk. English subtitles should let his manners and improvisation show through the disguise.

| Passage | Why it sticks; what English needs |
|---|---|
| **«Пасть порву! … Моргалы выколю!»** | Troshkin’s signature threats sound coarse, but their repetition exposes how few criminal phrases he knows. Use rough, forceful wording without making him sound effortlessly streetwise. |
| **«…пошью костюм с отливом и в Ялту!»** | Kosoy’s dream of wealth is a wonderfully specific Soviet-era wish list: a car, a shiny suit, a resort trip. Keep those modest, period-specific luxuries rather than replacing them with modern ones. |
| **«Всё! Кина не будет. Электричество кончилось.»** | A mock announcement that the fun is over; colloquial *кина* adds a deliberately folksy sound. Aim for a brisk, reusable line such as “That’s it—no movie. Power’s out.” |
| **«Так это в Турции, там тепло.»** | Troshkin responds to a cruel punishment proposal by worrying about the climate instead of the cruelty. Preserve the matter-of-fact non sequitur: “But that’s Turkey. It’s warm there.” |
| **«Нехороший человек? — Редиска.»** | The supposed underworld insult turns out to be “radish,” absurdly mild and kindergarten-friendly. Keep **“radish”**: a normal insult such as “jerk” erases the joke. |
| **«Фрей-фея, действительно: фрей-фея.»** | Troshkin has to check his notes for the alleged slang for a good person, puncturing his criminal persona. An unfamiliar-sounding “frey-feya” helps retain his uncertainty; a familiar English slang term would make him too competent. |
| **«…сарделька, сосиска, редиска, Навуходоносор, петух гамбургский!»** | His intimidation collapses into an escalating list of foods, a biblical king, and a rooster. Prioritize the improvised rhythm and mounting nonsense over making every item sound like a plausible insult. |
| **«Тут помню, тут… ничего.»** | Conveniently selective amnesia helps Troshkin avoid revealing the stolen helmet’s location. Keep his hesitation; the tidier catchphrase often quoted later—“I remember this bit, not that bit”—is not quite his on-screen wording. |
| **«Все побежали и я побежал.»** | Vasily explains joining an escape with disarming herd logic. “Everyone ran, so I ran too” needs no embellishment; its innocence is the point. |
| **«А в тюрьме сейчас ужин… макароны.»** | Hungry fugitives suddenly make prison dinner sound attractive. Retain the bleak reversal and the pause before the humble, enticing “pasta.” |
| **«Лошадью ходи, лошадью ходи, дурак!»** | An urgent chess instruction becomes funny through its blunt repetition and everyday “horse” rather than the proper chess term. “Move the horse!” retains more voice if the board is visible; “knight” offers clarity at that cost. |
| **«Не играть, не пить, не воровать без меня.»** | A moral-sounding prohibition flips into a gang leader’s rule. End on **“without me”**: “No gambling, no drinking, no stealing—without me.” |
| **«…как три тополя на Плющихе.»** | The trio standing conspicuously evokes the Soviet film *Three Poplars at Plyushchikha*. A literal subtitle preserves the image; a brief guide note can supply the film reference, which this film did not invent. |
| **«Кто же его посадит? Он же памятник!»** | The exchange turns on *посадить*, heard as both “seat” and “imprison”: “Who’d lock him up? He’s a monument!” English can keep the absurd answer, but a note is needed to recover the full pun. |
| **«Кушать подано! Садитесь жрать, пожалуйста.»** | Polite hosting crashes into a coarse command to eat. Mix registers—“Dinner is served! Sit down and stuff your faces, please”—rather than making the whole line either courteous or rude. |
| **«Ты туда не ходи, ты сюда ходи… снег башка попадёт…»** | Vasily’s earnest snow-warning is quotable for its insistent directions, heightened danger, and conspicuously nonstandard Russian. Suggest the broken syntax lightly; avoid turning his speech into a generic ethnic caricature. |
| **«Вежливость — лучшее оружие вора.»** | Troshkin recasts his natural courtesy as criminal strategy. Keep the compact, mock-serious aphorism: “Politeness is a thief’s best weapon.” |
| **«Украл, выпил — в тюрьму… Романтика!»** | The repeated cycle—steal, drink, prison—deflates Kosoy’s romantic picture of crime. Deliver the final “How romantic!” deadpan, not as genuine admiration. |
| **«Ес, ес, ОБХС.»** | A feeble English lesson slides from “yes, yes” into the sound of a Soviet economic-crime agency’s initials. Preserve the audible slide if possible, and explain the institutional reference in a guide note; the initials alone will mean little to English viewers. |
| **«Чем больше сдадим — тем лучше» / «Сдаёмсу-у…»** | Talk of turning things in pays off when the thieves turn in both the treasure and themselves. Allow the realization to land late, then stretch “We surrendeeer!” to match the drawn-out delivery. |

**Separate cultural reference:** «Проснись и пой» (“Wake Up and Sing”) is a song heard in the film, not a catchphrase coined by its characters.

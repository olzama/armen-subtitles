The film’s most reusable lines are often *winged phrases*—quotations people use jokingly in everyday speech—rather than internet memes. Across them, the key comic contrast is Ogurtsov’s solemn attempt to regulate a party.

| Passage | Why it sticks; what English must preserve |
|---|---|
| **03:19 — “We have a directive to celebrate New Year cheerfully.”** | Ogurtsov makes enjoyment sound compulsory. Use official-sounding language—“We’ve been instructed to have a good time”—rather than a friendly invitation. |
| **04:35; 35:28 — “Briefly—about forty minutes.”** | A stock joke about a speaker with no sense of brevity. Keep **“briefly”** and **“forty minutes”** side by side. |
| **05:14–05:27 — Cats and Baba Yaga** | He budgets fairy-tale characters as though they were employees. Keep the folklore and the staffing jargon: “We won’t hire a Baba Yaga from outside; we’ll train one in-house.” |
| **14:11–14:24 — “A big, mass-participation quartet.”** | His demand for scale defeats the meaning of *quartet*. That exact word must survive. |
| **16:21–16:41 — “The music should lead you, but not lead you away.”** | It sounds like artistic guidance but tells the musicians nothing. Preserve the repeated **lead**, not a sensible paraphrase. |
| **20:35 — “We need Gogols and Shchedrins!”** | Ogurtsov cites great satirists while trying to control satire. Retain the names; a note can identify them, but “comedians” loses the cultural and political bite. |
| **38:45–42:34 — “Five Minutes”** | A beloved New Year song more than a meme. Preserve the countdown to midnight and the emotional chance to speak or reconcile before the year ends; exact rhymes matter less. |
| **43:19 — “Sleight of hand—and no speech!”** | The magician makes Ogurtsov’s address vanish, twisting a familiar Russian magician’s formula. The scene makes the English line work without forcing an unfamiliar catchphrase. |
| **45:52–46:39 — Five stars; life on Mars** | The tipsy lecturer veers from stars in the sky to five-star cognac, then offers the famously useless conclusion that whether life exists on Mars is “unknown to science.” Make the bottle-rating joke legible and play the conclusion as an anticlimax. |
| **48:53–49:05 — “Pension-and-dance ensemble”** | A slip turns “song-and-dance” into “pension-and-dance,” apt for the retired performers. An audible self-correction carries the scene-specific pun. |
| **1:00:15–1:01:22 — “The Bear at the Ball”** | The fable plainly targets Ogurtsov, who praises its satire without recognizing himself. Preserve that indirect accusation and his absurd request to name the culprit **and workplace**. |
| **1:05:13–1:07:37 — “Your good mood will never leave you.”** | A fondly remembered refrain whose optimism is sincere—the opposite of Ogurtsov’s ordered cheer. Don’t subtitle it with a sarcastic edge. |

**Throughout:** preserve Ogurtsov’s pompous bureaucratic voice, but don’t convert every unusual pronunciation or subtitle typo into broken English.

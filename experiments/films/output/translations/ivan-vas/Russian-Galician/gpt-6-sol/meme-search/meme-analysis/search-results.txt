=== CHUNK 1 ===
These subtitles are from Leonid Gaidai’s 1973 comedy *Ivan Vasilievich Changes His Profession*. A time machine strands Ivan the Terrible in Soviet Moscow and sends his lookalike, building manager Bunsha, and a burglar into the tsar’s court. That reversal drives many of the jokes. **A celebrated film quotation is not necessarily an internet meme**; some lines below are both, while others are culturally familiar chiefly as quotations. A study of Russian-language memes documents how the film’s lines and images are reused and altered online. ([culture.ru](https://www.culture.ru/live/movies/2633/ivan-vasilevich-menyaet-professiyu?utm_source=openai))

| Subtitles | Passage and significance | What matters in Galician |
|---|---|---|
| **100–102, 13:43** | **«Это я удачно зашёл!»** (“I picked a good time to drop in!”). The burglar says it on finding an opportunity to steal. It is a reusable quip for stumbling into unexpected good fortune; the humour lies in *who* is pleased. ([maximonline.ru](https://www.maximonline.ru/entertainment/ivan-vasilevich-menyaet-professiyu-vse-citaty-iz-filma-id6551244/?utm_source=openai)) | Keep it breezy and self-satisfied, not ominous: *«Cheguei no momento xusto!»* |
| **101–102, 14:19** | **«Граждане, храните деньги в сберегательной кассе! Если, конечно, они у вас есть!»** The burglar delivers what sounds like a public-service banking slogan, then punctures it—and is himself the reason keeping money at home is risky. The line became prominent enough to be reused in a Sber advertising campaign. ([adindex.ru](https://adindex.ru/publication/opinion/marketing/2025/01/30/330423.phtml?utm_source=openai)) | Preserve **“citizens”**, the old-fashioned savings-bank phrasing and the delayed qualification: *«Cidadáns, gardade os cartos na caixa de aforros... se é que os tedes!»* |
| **147–149, 19:35; 569–570, 1:04:44** | **«Меня терзают смутные сомнения»** (“Vague suspicions torment me”). Bunsha’s grandiose way of saying “Something’s fishy” returns when he suspects another theft. Its repetition is part of the joke. ([skio.ru](https://skio.ru/afofilms/kino3.php?utm_source=openai)) | Use the **same** slightly pompous wording both times, such as *«Atorméntanme vagas sospeitas»*. |
| **152–153, 19:50** | **«Я — артист больших и малых академических театров! А фамилия моя слишком известная, чтобы я её называл».** The burglar evades a simple request for his name with absurd theatrical prestige. It is a recognisable quotation rather than a claim the viewer should believe. ([skio.ru](https://skio.ru/afofilms/kino3.php?utm_source=openai)) | Keep the reversal “large and small” and the inflated register; don’t supply a name. |
| **199–200, 25:34–25:43** | **«Замуровали, демоны!» / «Вот что крест животворящий делает!»** The displaced tsar interprets a technological mishap through religion and demons. Both are recorded as catchphrases from the film; the second also appears in Bulgakov’s earlier play. ([info.wikireading.ru](https://info.wikireading.ru/223797)) | *Замуровали* means **“They’ve walled me in!”**, not merely “They’ve shut the door.” Retain the tsar’s religious vocabulary. |
| **202, 26:13** | **«Закусывать надо!»** A curt response to someone apparently having trouble with drink: “You should eat something with it!” It relies on the practice of accompanying spirits with *zakuska*—food eaten between drinks. ([maximonline.ru](https://www.maximonline.ru/entertainment/ivan-vasilevich-menyaet-professiyu-vse-citaty-iz-filma-id6551244/?utm_source=openai)) | A short *«Hai que comer algo!»* works if the drinking context is visible. Do not turn it into general medical advice. |
| **230, 29:20** | **«Ключница водку делала?»** The tsar approves of the vodka as though a palace household servant had made it. The question is one of his especially remembered lines. ([culture.ru](https://www.culture.ru/live/movies/2633/ivan-vasilevich-menyaet-professiyu?utm_source=openai)) | *Ключница* is a woman in charge of household stores or keys, **not a locksmith**. A concise subtitle could use *«Fíxoa a encargada da despensa?»* if “vodka” remains clear from context. |
| **336–337, 41:47; 352–358, 43:25** | **«Царя, царя!» — «Шайбу, шайбу!»**; later **«Пусть на обратном пути Казань возьмут. Ну, чтоб два раза не ездить»**. The first turns a crowd calling for the tsar into a modern ice-hockey chant (“Puck! Puck!”). The second treats a military campaign like an errand to combine with another trip—only to learn Kazan has already been taken. ([kinomoment.ru](https://kinomoment.ru/movie/42664/quotes/?utm_source=openai)) | For the chant, prioritise an audible, crowd-like switch of context (*«O tsar!» — «O disco!»*), even if the original rhythm cannot fully survive. For Kazan, keep the deadpan practical excuse: *«...para non teren que facer a viaxe dúas veces»*. |
| **397, 49:23** | **«Жди меня, и я вернусь».** This is not a saying invented by the film: it invokes the opening of Konstantin Simonov’s famous wartime poem. Here the solemn romantic promise is used in a chaotic farce. ([culture.ru](https://www.culture.ru/poems/32975/zhdi-menya-i-ya-vernus?utm_source=openai)) | Translate simply—*«Espérame e volverei»*—but flag the **poetic allusion** in a translator’s note if notes are available. |
| **421–428, 51:26–51:57** | **«Бориску на царство?»** The real tsar’s outraged speech about Boris is mistaken for a superb acting audition. “Boriska” is a belittling form of Boris; the line is also catalogued as a film catchphrase with a counterpart in Bulgakov’s play. ([info.wikireading.ru](https://info.wikireading.ru/223797)) | Keep the diminutive insult and the contrast between genuine fury and the director’s delighted *“Bravo!”*: *«O Borisiño, no trono?»* is one possible approach. |
| **477–486, 55:27–56:20** | **«Аз есмь. Житие моё»; «Паки, паки… Иже херувимы!»; «Красотою лепа…»** The director desperately strings together words that *sound* old or liturgical when confronted with Ivan; Ivan’s ornate praise of the woman is in a markedly different register from Soviet conversation. The pseudo-archaic panic is the joke, not a coherent speech in a foreign language. ([yiv1999.narod.ru](https://yiv1999.narod.ru/IvanVasilevich.htm?utm_source=openai)) | Give the Galician deliberately archaic or liturgical *colour* without making the director’s nonsense fluent. Distinguish his fumbling from the tsar’s assured, courtly style. |
| **517–518; 583, 59:00 and 1:06:33** | **«Красота-то какая! Лепота!»** “What beauty—*lepota*!” is both a famous film exclamation and a documented visual-internet-meme source. *Лепота* sounds quaint beside everyday *красота*. ([info.wikireading.ru](https://info.wikireading.ru/223797)) | Don’t flatten the two words into identical “beautiful”s. Consider *«Que fermosura! Que beldade!»*, with a slightly old-fashioned second beat. |
| **533–552, 1:01:42–1:03:12** | **«Гитлер капут!»; «Кемскую волость?!»; «Такие вопросы… с кондачка не решаются».** Facing a Swedish envoy, Bunsha offers the only “German” he can muster: a Second World War slogan. He is then ready to give away the Kem territory casually, while Miloslavsky intervenes. The clash of sixteenth-century diplomacy, Soviet speech and improvised foreign languages makes this sequence memorable. ([m-bulgakov.ru](https://m-bulgakov.ru/ekranizacii/ivan-vasilevich-menjaet-professiju?utm_source=openai)) | Leave **«Hitler kaput!»** recognisable. Treat *волость* as a historical district or territory, not a modern province. For *с кондачка*, use “lightly” or “off the cuff”: *«Un asunto así non se decide á lixeira»*. |
| **576–581, 1:05:32–1:06:05; 751–753, 1:21:38** | **«За чей счёт этот банкет?»** (“Who’s paying for this banquet?”) and **«Икра чёрная, красная… заморская — баклажанная!»**. Bureaucratic anxiety interrupts a royal feast. The caviar reveal gives lavish emphasis to an eggplant spread after real black and red caviar; that incongruity made it a food-related catchphrase and meme. ([culture.ru](https://www.culture.ru/live/movies/2633/ivan-vasilevich-menyaet-professiyu?utm_source=openai)) | Preserve the pause and anticlimax: *«Caviar negro, vermello... e o de ultramar: de berenxena!»* The last item is a **vegetable spread**, not fish roe. |
| **588–597, 1:06:43–1:07:12; 780–782, 1:24:45** | **«Всё, что нажито непосильным трудом, всё погибло!»** Shpak laments his stolen possessions, later multiplying the quantities in his account. The formula is a recognised catchphrase and occurs in Bulgakov’s play; in context, his catalogue also invites suspicion about his wealth and honesty. ([info.wikireading.ru](https://info.wikireading.ru/223797)) | *«Todo o que conseguín con tanto traballo... desapareceu!»* Keep the aggrieved self-pity and, crucially, the **changing numbers**. |
| **597, 1:07:06** | **«Оставь меня, старушка, я в печали».** The tsar’s dignified “Leave me, old woman; I am grieving” lands badly because the woman takes *старушка* personally. It is cited among his signature lines. ([culture.ru](https://www.culture.ru/live/movies/2633/ivan-vasilevich-menyaet-professiyu?utm_source=openai)) | Do not soften away **“old woman”**: her offended reply depends on it. |
| **708, 1:15:40; 711–712, 1:17:40** | **«Танцуют все!» / «Я требую продолжения банкета!»** The first commands everyone to dance; the second, delivered as the impostor’s situation collapses, demands that the party continue. Both are catalogued catchphrases and have life as reusable party jokes or memes. ([info.wikireading.ru](https://info.wikireading.ru/223797)) | Keep them brisk and quotable: *«A bailar todo o mundo!»* and *«Esixo que continúe o banquete!»* |
| **732–735, 1:20:14–1:20:29** | **«Тамбовский волк тебе боярин!» / «Казань брал, Астрахань брал, Ревель брал, Шпака не брал».** The policeman twists the established put-down “The Tambov wolf is your comrade, not I” to answer Ivan’s **boyar**; Ivan then answers a question about whether he “took” Shpak’s flat as though listing captured cities. The second line is a classic reusable film quotation. ([journals.tsutmb.ru](https://journals.tsutmb.ru/a8/upload/auto/5a/ac/temp.5aac3adba6231988a69cc50bdff3fc76.pdf?utm_source=openai)) | The first means **“Don’t call me boyar”**, not that a wolf is present. For the second, find a Galician verb that can plausibly span taking a city and taking a flat—*«Tomei Kazán… o piso de Shpak, non»*. |
| **791, 795–797, 1:25:39–1:26:12** | **«И тебя вылечат! И тебя тоже вылечат! И меня вылечат!»** When his wife cannot distinguish the two Ivans, “They’ll cure you too” comes back to implicate herself. The escalating repetition is a particularly well-attested catchphrase. ([info.wikireading.ru](https://info.wikireading.ru/223797)) | Repeat **the same verb** each time—*«E a ti hante curar… E a min tamén!»*—rather than varying synonyms. |
| **17–33; 361–379; 693–708** | **The songs**: *«Звенит январская вьюга»*, *«Маруся»* (“Кап-кап-кап”), and *«Счастье вдруг…»* are culturally significant film songs, not merely background dialogue. The meme study specifically finds online reuse of a line from *«Звенит январская вьюга»*. ([philology-journal.ru](https://philology-journal.ru/article/phil20240175/pdf)) | Mark them as **singing** and preserve each song’s refrain and tone. For ordinary viewing subtitles, clarity and recurrence matter more than forcing a singable Galician metre. |

**Overall translation principle:** retain the film’s collisions of registers—tsarist ceremony, Soviet bureaucracy, modern slang, liturgical fragments and show-business chatter. A uniformly “historical” Galician translation would lose the joke; so would one that modernised every voice. The repeated wording, pauses and abrupt changes of register are often what make these passages quotable.

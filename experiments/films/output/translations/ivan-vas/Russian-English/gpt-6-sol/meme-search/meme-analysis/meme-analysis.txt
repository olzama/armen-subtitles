## Meme translation guide: *Ivan Vasilievich Changes Profession*

Most of these are **film catchphrases that later became meme captions**, not jokes invented online. Their staying power comes from short, reusable wording—and from a tsar, a Soviet building manager and a burglar speaking in sharply different registers. The English lines below are possible solutions, not established subtitles.

| Passage | Why it sticks; what the translation needs |
|---|---|
| **«Это я удачно зашёл!»** | Miloslavsky’s delighted “I picked the right time to drop in!” turns an accidental opportunity into a cheerful personal triumph. Keep it brief and opportunistic. |
| **«Граждане, храните деньги в сберегательной кассе! Если, конечно, они у вас есть!»** | A burglar delivers a mock public-service announcement while stealing. Retain the official-sounding **“Citizens”** and land the sting last: “if you have any.” |
| **«Меня терзают смутные сомнения…»** | Bunsha’s decorous way of voicing an increasingly obvious suspicion works as a reaction caption. “I’m beginning to have a nagging suspicion…” preserves the hesitant understatement; repeat its wording for the later callback. |
| **«Замуровали! … демоны!» / «Вот что крест животворящий делает!»** | Ivan reads a time-machine mishap as demonic action, then credits his cross when events change. His sincere terror and solemn religious diction must contrast with the visible technology. The worldly **«Закусывать надо!»** suggests he needs food *with his drink*, not a casual snack. |
| **«Пусть на обратном пути Казань возьмут. Ну, чтоб два раза не ездить»** | The false tsar schedules a conquest like an errand: “Take Kazan on the way back—save a second trip.” Keep the absurd practicality, not military grandeur. |
| **«Бориску на царство?»** | The real tsar’s contemptuous outrage is mistaken by a director for splendid acting. “*Boris* on the throne?” needs a scornful delivery; the Russian name form is insulting, not affectionate. |
| **«Я. Аз есмь. Житие моё» / «Паки, паки… иже херувимы!»** | Yakin bluffs with scraps of archaic and church language. It is **nonsense meant to sound learned**: use a few marked, archaic-sounding fragments, not fluent biblical English. |
| **«Ох, красота-то какая! Лепота!»** | Ivan’s wonder at modern Moscow culminates in the distinctive old-fashioned **лепота**. “What splendour!” conveys more of its quotable tsar-like flavour than a plain “Lovely!” |
| **«Кемскую волость… пусть забирают» / «…с кондачка не решаются»** | The impostor nearly gives away a district; Miloslavsky rescues him with “We can’t decide that off the cuff.” Preserve the territorial stakes and the comic switch to Soviet-style consultation and delay. |
| **«Икра чёрная, красная… заморская, баклажанная!»** | Lavish fish roe is announced routinely; humble eggplant spread gets the grand “exotic” reveal. “Black caviar, red caviar… and the exotic delicacy: eggplant ‘caviar’!” keeps both the ceremony and the food-word joke. |
| **«За чей счёт этот банкет?» / «Я требую продолжения банкета!»** | One line asks who pays for extravagance; the other demands that the fun go on regardless. Bunsha’s anxiety about the bill is especially funny while he is posing as tsar. Keep both lines punchy enough to reuse. |
| **«Оставь меня, старушка, я в печали»** | Pompous self-pity meets an insult to his wife, who objects that she is younger than he is. “Leave me be, old woman; I am in mourning” retains the grand manner and gives her something to protest. |
| **«Аз есмь царь» / «Казань брал, Астрахань брал, Ревель брал, Шпака не брал»** | At a police interview, Ivan confuses taking Shpak’s apartment with taking cities. Use **“took”** throughout if possible—“I took Kazan… I did not take Shpak”—so English listeners can hear the same misunderstanding. His “I am the tsar” should sound archaic and emphatic. |
| **«Всё, что нажил непосильным трудом…» / «И тебя вылечат… и меня вылечат!»** | Shpak’s claim that his possessions cost him “backbreaking work” is comically self-serving. The second joke is Bunsha’s wife losing confidence in everyone’s sanity, including her own: preserve the escalating **“you… you… me!”** |
| **«Торопиться не надо. Сесть я всегда успею»** | Told to sit and write a dissertation, the burglar hears **сесть** as “go to prison.” English cannot easily share the verb; “No rush. I can always do time later” preserves the character-based punchline. |

### Shorter passages and recurring register jokes

- **Zina’s departure; «Ты меня пока не выписывай».** Romance and missing gloves receive similarly grave treatment, while Shurik minds his machine. Her request means **don’t remove her apartment residence registration yet** in case the affair fails; make that practical contingency clear.
- **«Это дело общественное» / «соответствующих органов».** Bunsha treats divorce as a threat to building statistics and invention as a matter for “the appropriate authorities.” His officious language—not the literal wording alone—is the joke.
- **«Я — артист больших и малых академических театров!»** Miloslavsky avoids giving his name with impossibly grand theatrical credentials. Translate it as an obvious, agile bluff.
- **«Положи трубку!» / «Ложи трубку!»** The second command is nonstandard Russian. If the exchange is being foregrounded, let the English become rougher or less polished rather than repeating an identical line.
- **«Повторяй…»** Bunsha repeats “Repeat after me” itself. This one transfers directly: “Repeat after me.” / “Repeat after me.” / “Not *that* part!”
- **«Были демоны… но они самоликвидировались» / «Шайбу! Шайбу!»** “The demons have eliminated themselves” applies deadpan bureaucracy to the supernatural. The hockey-crowd cry “Score! Score!” is funny because it erupts in a royal court.
- **«Святой человек со всеми удобствами» / «Красотою лепа…».** Zina describes a “saintly man with all modern conveniences,” borrowing apartment-ad language; Ivan praises beauty in an archaic register. Neither should become ordinary romantic speech.
- **«Гитлер капут!»** Bunsha’s irrelevant scrap of wartime German is all he can offer a German-speaking envoy. Its historical familiarity and total unsuitability matter more than translating each word.
- **«Нам ли быть в печали?» / «Танцуют все!»** The impostor turns a royal feast into a buoyant modern party. “Why should *we* be gloomy?” / “Everybody dance!” should sound like exuberant commands.
- **«Тамбовский волк тебе боярин!»** The officer reshapes a familiar Russian “Tambov wolf” rebuff after Ivan calls him *boyar*. “I’m no nobleman of yours!” gives the immediate meaning; retaining the wolf needs room for a less transparent cultural joke.

### Songs and borrowed lines

**«Звенит январская вьюга»** and **«Счастье вдруг…»** are recognisable songs associated with the film, not internet-origin punchlines. Subtitle them as lyrics; only a singable adaptation needs to privilege metre. **«Поговори хоть ты со мной, подруга семиструнная»** addresses a *seven-string guitar* on a recording. **«Жди меня, и я вернусь»** borrows the opening of Konstantin Simonov’s already-famous wartime poem: its resonance predates the film.

**Rule of thumb:** protect the collision of voices. Ivan’s archaic conviction, Bunsha’s administrative reflexes and Miloslavsky’s quick-witted opportunism usually carry the meme effect more reliably than a word-for-word rendering.

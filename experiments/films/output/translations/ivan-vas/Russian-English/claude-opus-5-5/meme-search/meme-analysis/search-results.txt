=== CHUNK 1 ===
I've identified the film as **Иван Васильевич меняет профессию** (*Ivan Vasilievich Changes Profession*, Gaidai, 1973, based on Bulgakov's play *Ivan Vasilievich*). Almost every scene has a line Russians still quote. The ones that matter most for an English translator are below, in film order. Subtitle numbers are in brackets.

### Main memes

| # | Line | Context and what a translator should know |
|---|---|---|
| 16 | **Но риск, как говорится…** | The speaker stops halfway through a well-known saying, "риск — благородное дело" ("risk is a noble business"). Russian viewers fill in the rest. The English needs a saying that can also be cut off and still recognised, for example "Nothing ventured…". |
| 36 | **Три раза я разводилась… Зюзина я не считаю** | She says she's been divorced three times, then adds that she doesn't count Zyuzin. It's a deadpan joke about her love life and is often quoted. |
| 57 | **А он: «так-так-так»** | Shurik answers "так-так-так" when his wife says she's leaving. In English this is a distracted "uh-huh, uh-huh". The line has to stay short enough to sound rude. |
| 85–86 | **Будь я вашей женой… — …я бы повесился!** | This mirrors the famous Lady Astor/Churchill exchange ("If I were your husband, I'd drink it"). English viewers will know the pattern, so a wording that echoes it helps. |
| 100 | **Это я удачно зашёл!** | The burglar Miloslavsky says this when he sees the loot. It's a major meme for any lucky find. Use something short and smug, such as "Well, didn't I drop in at the right time." |
| 101–102 | **Граждане, храните деньги в сберегательной кассе! Если, конечно, они у вас есть** | This quotes a well-known Soviet savings-bank slogan, then mocks it. English viewers won't know the slogan, so the advertising tone has to carry the joke. |
| 147 | **На мне узоров нету и цветы не растут** | It means "Why are you staring? I'm not a painting." It's a folksy brush-off. |
| 148, 569 | **Меня терзают смутные сомнения** | "I am tormented by vague doubts." It's a stock phrase for being suspicious. The first time it's about Shpak's suede jacket. The second time it sets up "Ты на что, царская морда, намекаешь?" ("What are you hinting at, you royal mug?"). Keep the wording the same both times so the callback works. |
| 158 | **Вы на мне дыру протрёте** | An idiom: "You'll stare a hole through me." English has the same phrase. |
| 199 | **Замуровали, демоны!** | "They've walled me in, the demons!" This is one of the best-known quotes. People use it whenever they're stuck in a lift, a room or a situation. The tsar's high, archaic panic matters more than the exact words. |
| 200 | **Вот что крест животворящий делает!** | "That's what the life-giving cross can do!" Used ironically when something seems to work like magic. Keep the religious register. |
| 201–202 | **А где царь? — Закусывать надо!** | Someone asks where the tsar is, and the answer is that you have to eat something with your vodka. This drinking rule is a running theme. |
| 230 | **Ключница водку делала?** | "Did the housekeeper make the vodka?" The tsar can't imagine factory-made vodka. A ключница was the housekeeper who kept the keys. |
| 236 | **Ты меня уважаешь?** | "Do you respect me?" The film didn't invent this. It's the classic Russian drunk's line, used to pressure someone into drinking. Russians recognise it at once, and the tsar saying it is the joke. |
| 256–257 | **Головы им отрубят и всего делов. — Да пёс с ними!** | "They'll cut their heads off, that's all." "Oh, to hell with them!" The humour is in how casually Shurik shrugs it off. |
| 271 | **Ой, не похож! Ой, халтура!** | "Oh, he doesn't look like him! What a botch job!" It's a theatre/film in-joke. Халтура means shoddy, hack work. |
| 273 | **Вот лица попрошу не касаться!** | "Kindly leave my face out of this!" Bunsha's prim bureaucratic tone is the point. |
| 289–293 | **Царь тут… Царь всегда на своём месте!** | "The tsar's here… The tsar is always in his place." |
| 294 etc. | **Не вели казнить** (Feofan, played by Kramarov) | The full formula is "Не вели казнить, вели слово молвить" ("Don't have me executed, let me say a word"). Russians use it jokingly before raising an awkward topic. Translators usually render it as "Spare me, Sire!" |
| 298 | **Были демоны… но они самоликвидировались** | "There were demons, we don't deny it. But they liquidated themselves." Soviet official language is used for a supernatural event. |
| 302–303 | **Короче говоря, все вон. — Рявкни на них! — Вон!** | "In short, everybody out." "Bark at them!" "OUT!" |
| 304–305 | **Довольно кувыркаться! Кинулся раз, кинулся два — хватит!** | "Enough tumbling about! You threw yourself down once, twice, that'll do!" |
| 336 | **Шайбу, шайбу…** | "Puck, puck!" was the standard Soviet hockey crowd chant. The crowd outside shouting "Царя!" ("The tsar!") reminds Miloslavsky of it. An English equivalent would be "Defence! Defence!" |
| 351 | **Я не имею права подписывать такие исторические документы** | "I have no right to sign such historic documents." Bunsha's bureaucratic fear of signing anything. Often quoted. |
| 353–358 | **Пусть на обратном пути Казань возьмут… Чтоб два раза не ездить** | "Have them take Kazan on the way back… saves a second trip." People still say "чтоб два раза не ездить" ("so we don't have to go twice"). For the history joke to land, English viewers need to know Ivan IV conquered Kazan in 1552. |
| 380 | **Ну, пошли дела кой-как** | "Well, things are getting going, more or less." |
| 405 | **Этого святого человека со всеми удобствами** | "That saint of a man, with all mod cons." It's a famous line because she describes her husband like a flat for rent. "Со всеми удобствами" is estate-agent language for "with all amenities." |
| 411 | **Профессьон де фуа!** | Zina mangles the French "profession de foi" (profession of faith). It's a would-be-classy word used wrongly, so keep it garbled. |
| 428 | **Ты пошто боярыню обидел, смерд?!** | "Why hast thou wronged the boyar's lady, peasant?" A big meme, quoted as mock-archaic outrage. Пошто/по что is an old word for "why", and смерд means peasant/serf. Use English mock-archaic: "Why hast thou…, knave?" |
| 431–435 | **Бондарчук? Никулин? …Смоктуновский! Кеша!** | Yakin guesses which famous Soviet actors are in the make-up. English viewers won't know the names. Keep them, or think about a gloss in the subtitle. |
| 454–461 | **Ты чьих будешь? … Эта роль ругательная, я прошу её ко мне не применять** | "Whose are you?" means "whose serf are you?" Bunsha takes the insult холоп (serf/slave) personally: "That role is abusive, kindly don't apply it to me." The joke is the clash between feudal and Soviet language. |
| 462–463 | **Боремся за почётное звание дома высокой культуры быта** | "And we're competing for the honorary title of 'House of High Living Standards.'" This mocks Soviet socialist-competition titles, and comes back at #783. |
| 477–481 | **Аз есмь. Житие моё. — Какое житие твоё, пёс смердящий? … Паки, паки… иже херувимы** | Yakin tries to sound like Church Slavonic by putting together random liturgical words, roughly "I am. My life." The tsar answers "What 'life of yours', you stinking dog?" Use fake King James English: "Verily, verily… thee… cherubim." |
| 485 | **Красотою лепа, червлена губами, бровьми союзна** | Archaic praise of a woman's beauty: fair, red-lipped, with joined eyebrows. In old Russia, eyebrows meeting in the middle counted as beautiful. |
| 495 | **Жалую тебе шубу с царского плеча** | "I grant you a fur coat from the tsar's own shoulder." It's a real idiom for a generous gift from someone powerful, and it's often used ironically. |
| 517, 583 | **Красота-то какая! Лепота!** | "What beauty! Splendour!" Huge meme. Лепота is an archaic word for beauty and is now used jokingly for anything pleasant. Pick one archaic word, such as "Fairness!" or "Comeliness!", and use it the same way each time. |
| 523–524 | **Введите гражданина посла! — Сдвинь брови!** | "Show in Citizen Ambassador!" "Frown!" |
| 533 | **Гитлер капут!** | This was the only German many Soviet people knew, a war-film cliché. Leave it as is. |
| 535, 550 | **Кемска волост!** | The ambassador's Swedish accent drops the soft sign. Kem County (Кемская волость) is a real area in Karelia. Russians use the phrase ironically about giving territory away. Keep the broken accent in English ("Kemska volost!"). |
| 541–543 | **Да пусть забирают на здоровье! … Государство не обеднеет** | "Let them take it, by all means! The state won't go broke." |
| 549 | **Передай твой король мой пламенный привет** | "Give your king my fiery greetings." He copies the ambassador's broken grammar, and "пламенный привет" is Soviet greeting language. Keep the bad grammar: "Give your king my fiery greeting." |
| 551–552 | **Такие вопросы с кондачка не решаются. Надо посоветоваться с товарищами. Зайдите на недельке** | "Such questions aren't decided off the cuff. We need to consult the comrades. Come back next week." This is Soviet bureaucratic stalling and is still quoted. Use English bureaucratese, for example "We'll need to consult with colleagues." |
| 555–556 | **Ауфвидерзеен! Гудбай! Оревуар! Короче говоря, чао!** | "Auf Wiedersehen! Goodbye! Au revoir! In short, ciao!" |
| 577–578 | **За чей счёт этот банкет? — Во всяком случае, не мы!** | "Who's paying for this banquet?" "Not us, anyway!" |
| 579–581 | **Икра чёрная, красная… Заморская икра… баклажанная!** | Feofan lists black and red caviar, then "overseas caviar" from the Soviet side: aubergine spread, a cheap Soviet staple. The poor modern food set against royal luxury is the joke. A translation like "Aubergine caviar" or "eggplant dip" will need the build-up. |
| 582 | **Ну, царь, вздрогнули!** | "Well, Tsar, bottoms up!" Вздрогнули ("let's shudder") is a Soviet drinking-slang toast. |
| 587–588, 780–783 | **Всё, что нажито непосильным трудом… куртка замшевая — три** | Shpak's famous list of what was "earned by back-breaking toil." The joke is that the list grows each time: two tape recorders, then three, then three suede jackets. Keep the numbers and list order exactly. |
| 597 | **Оставь меня, старушка, я в печали** | "Leave me, old woman, I am grieving." A stylised, pseudo-poetic line that's often quoted. |
| 614 | **— Смерд! — От смерда слышу!** | "Peasant!" "Takes one to know one!" This is the Russian schoolyard comeback "от … слышу". It comes back as "От самозванца слышу" ("Impostor yourself!") at #788. |
| 645–646 | **Очень приятно, царь!** | Bunsha's flustered, repeated greeting. A top meme. |
| 659–667 | **Нам, царям, за вредность надо молоко бесплатно давать … Нервные клетки не восстанавливаются** | Soviet workers in hazardous jobs got free milk. Bunsha asks for the same perk for tsars. "Nerve cells don't regenerate" is a popular Soviet health saying. |
| 668 | **Аки пчела** | "Like a bee." Archaic for a hard worker. |
| 686 | **Это нам не тили-тили, это мы не трали-вали** | Bunsha garbles a Mikhalkov children's song ("Это мы не проходили, это нам не задавали…"). This is from memory, not checked. It's nonsense filler, so English nonsense will do. |
| 708 | **Танцуют все!** | "Everybody dance!" Very well known. |
| 712 | **Я требую продолжения банкета!** | "I demand the banquet continue!" This is perhaps the film's most quoted line. Keep the formal "demand." |
| 722–728 | **Аз есмь царь. — Кличка! … Тарапунька и Штепсель нашёлся** | "I am the tsar." "Is that a nickname?" Кличка is criminal slang for an alias. Tarapunka and Shtepsel were a popular Ukrainian Soviet comedy duo, so the English could use a comedy-double-act reference. |
| 732 | **Тамбовский волк тебе боярин!** | This twists the criminal-slang put-down "Тамбовский волк тебе товарищ" ("The Tambov wolf is your comrade," meaning "I'm no comrade of yours"). It's a famous line. English has no fixed equivalent, so something like "Boyar, my foot!" is needed. |
| 735 | **Казань брал, Астрахань брал, Ревель брал, Шпака не брал** | Брать means both "to conquer" and "to rob/burgle." It's the film's best pun and needs a verb that covers both meanings: "Took Kazan, took Astrakhan, took Reval… never took Shpak." "Took" works in English, even better with "did not take." |
| 795–797 | **И тебя вылечат… И меня вылечат** | "They'll cure you too… They'll cure me too." Short and cheerful; often quoted. |
| 800 | **Сесть я всегда успею** | Сесть means both "to sit down" (to write the dissertation) and "to go to prison." Possible English: "I'll be doing time soon enough," set up by "sit down to write." |
| 813 | **В милицию замели. Дело шьют!** | "Got hauled in by the police. They're stitching me up!" This is criminal slang coming from the tsar's mouth. Use English slang such as "They're trying to frame me." |

### Other references
- **Songs.** "Звенит январская вьюга" ("The January blizzard rings") and "Разговор со счастьем" ("Счастье вдруг в тишине") are well-known Zatsepin/Derbenev hits. "Кап-кап-кап" parodies Soviet film-studio pseudo-folk.
- **#397 «Жди меня, и я вернусь».** The first line of Simonov's famous WWII poem ("Wait for me, and I'll return"), said here with pompous irony.
- **#329 «Ванька-разбойник… а я наоборот, Жорж».** "Vanka the Bandit… and I'm the opposite, Georges." The joke is that a plain Russian Vanya is the "opposite" of the posh French Georges. In Gogol's *The Government Inspector*, the impostor Khlestakov claims he wrote the novel *Yuri Miloslavsky*. That may be why Bulgakov chose the name, but this is my guess and I didn't check it.
- **Throughout: Soviet officialese.** Bunsha speaks in it ("соответствующие органы" = "the relevant authorities", "показатели" = "performance figures", "представитель общественности" = "representative of the community"). Use matching English bureaucratic language so the clash with the tsar's archaic speech survives.
- **#189–194 «Положь / ложи трубку».** "Put the phone down." Положь and ложи are uneducated verb forms, and #194 «Положи» corrects them. Russians notice this register difference. In English it shows only through tone.

### English titles
The film is known as *Ivan Vasilievich: Back to the Future* and as *Ivan Vasilievich Changes Profession*. Mosfilm's official upload on YouTube has English subtitles, which would make a useful baseline.

Loose ends:
- I couldn't find a documented history of how "Кемска волость" spread online. The territory-joke use comes from my own knowledge.
- The sources for "Тамбовский волк" don't agree on where it comes from (criminal/Gulag slang is the most likely). That doesn't change the translation.
- The Mikhalkov source for #686 and the Khlestakov link for Miloslavsky are from memory and unchecked.
- I couldn't access the 17th-century source article for "Не вели казнить" (HTTP 401).

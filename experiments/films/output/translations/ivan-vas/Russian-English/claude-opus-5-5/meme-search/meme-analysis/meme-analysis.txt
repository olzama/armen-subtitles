# Meme Translation Guide: *Ivan Vasilievich Changes Profession* (RU → EN)

**Global strategy.** Most of the comedy comes from clashing registers. Ivan uses archaic Church-Slavonic court speech. Bunsha uses Soviet officialese. Miloslavsky uses crook's slang. The English needs three equally distinct voices: mock-King-James for Ivan, committee bureaucratese for Bunsha, and con-man patter for Miloslavsky. When a meme line is untranslatable, keep the register right and accept losing the exact words.

## Lines, in film order

- **16 «Но риск, как говорится…»** The audience completes the proverb in their heads, and that is the joke. Use a proverb English viewers can complete from its first half: *"But nothing ventured, as they say…"*
- **36 «Три раза… Зюзина я не считаю»** The humour is deadpan understatement: one husband simply doesn't count. Keep it flat and offhand: *"Three times. I don't count Zyuzin."* Don't add any explanation.
- **57 «А он: так-так-так»** A man absorbed in his work answers his wife's departure with distracted noises. Use *"Right, right, right"* or *"Uh-huh, uh-huh."* It must stay as short and absent-minded as the Russian.
- **85–86 «Будь я вашей женой… — я бы повесился!»** Bunsha says that if he were Shurik's wife he'd leave too. Shurik answers that if Bunsha were his wife, he'd hang himself. The pattern matches the Astor/Churchill retort, so mirror its wording: *"If I were your wife…" / "If you were my wife, I'd hang myself!"*
- **100 «Это я удачно зашёл!»** People quote it about any lucky find. The tone is smug and casual: *"Well, didn't I drop by at the right time."*
- **101–102 «Храните деньги в сберегательной кассе!»** He recites a real Soviet savings-bank slogan straight to camera, then undercuts it. English viewers won't know the slogan, so render it in recognisable public-service-ad style (*"Citizens! Keep your savings in the State Savings Bank!"*) and let *"…if you have any, that is"* deliver the punchline.
- **147 «На мне узоров нету и цветы не растут»** A folksy way of saying "stop staring." Keep the image rather than reducing it to a neutral line: *"I've no patterns on me and no flowers growing."*
- **148 / 569 «Меня терзают смутные сомнения»** It is quoted as a mock-grand way to say "I'm suspicious." The second time it comes with the rhyme «у Шпака магнитофон, у посла медальон». Use identical wording both times (*"I am tormented by vague misgivings"*), and keep a rhyme in the follow-up if possible (*"Shpak's lost a tape machine, the envoy's locket's not been seen"*).
- **158 «Вы на мне дыру протрёте»** Direct equivalent: *"You'll stare a hole through me."*
- **199 «Замуровали, демоны!»** This is the tsar's archaic panic at a modern locked flat. People quote it whenever they feel trapped. The archaic exclamation is what makes it work: *"Walled me in, the fiends!"* The word "fiends" or "demons" must sound medieval, not like a modern curse.
- **200 «Вот что крест животворящий делает!»** Used ironically when something seems to work by magic. Keep the liturgical phrase: *"Behold the power of the life-giving Cross!"*
- **201–202 «Закусывать надо!»** This is the drinking rule of always eating something after vodka, and it recurs through the film. The answer is a non-sequitur to "where's the tsar?" Use *"Should've had a bite with it!"* and phrase it the same way each time it recurs.
- **230 «Ключница водку делала?»** The tsar can only imagine vodka made at home by the household. *"Did the housekeeper brew this?"* works. "Housekeeper" or "stewardess of the keys" is enough.
- **236 «Ты меня уважаешь?»** This is the standard Russian drunk's way of pressuring someone to drink, and it is funny in the tsar's mouth. English has no fixed equivalent. *"Do you respect me?"* still reads as drunk talk if it is delivered with the same insistence.
- **256–257 «Головы отрубят и всего делов. — Да пёс с ними!»** The joke is how casually they accept beheadings. Keep both lines breezy: *"They'll chop their heads off, that's all." "Ah, the hell with them."*
- **271 «Ой, не похож! Ой, халтура!»** A film-industry person reviews the "tsar" as if he were a bad casting choice. *"Doesn't look a bit like him! Hack job!"*
- **273 «Вот лица попрошу не касаться!»** Bunsha takes a remark about his face as an official complaint. Use stiff committee English: *"I'll ask you to leave my face out of this!"*
- **294 «Не вели казнить…»** This is the formula for raising something risky with a superior, and the full version continues «вели слово молвить». *"Spare me, Sire!"* loses the second half. If there is room, use *"Spare my head, Sire, and hear my word!"*
- **298 «Были демоны… но они самоликвидировались»** An official report on something supernatural. Keep the bureaucratic verb: *"There were demons, we don't deny it. But they have self-liquidated."*
- **302–305 «Все вон… Рявкни! — Вон!» / «Довольно кувыркаться!»** Bunsha has to be coached to bark orders, and he scolds the boyars' prostrations as if they were gymnastics. *"Enough somersaulting! Flung yourselves down once, twice. That'll do!"*
- **336 «Шайбу, шайбу…»** The crowd chants «Царя!» and Miloslavsky hears a hockey stadium. "Defence!" makes little sense here. A better fit: crowd *"We want the tsar!"*, Miloslavsky *"We want a goal!"*
- **351 «Не имею права подписывать такие исторические документы»** A Soviet functionary's fear of signing anything. *"I'm not authorised to sign historic documents."* The word "authorised" carries the bureaucratic flavour.
- **353–358 «Пусть на обратном пути Казань возьмут… чтоб два раза не ездить»** The meme is the phrase «чтоб два раза не ездить», the logic of combining errands, here applied to conquest. *"…so we don't have to make two trips"* lands without any history lesson. Kazan also sets up line 735.
- **397 «Жди меня, и я вернусь»** This is the first line of Simonov's war poem, spoken with mock pathos. Keep it as a solemn line ("Wait for me, and I'll return"). Some English viewers may know it, and the solemn tone alone does the work.
- **405 «Со всеми удобствами»** She describes her husband like a flat for rent. Use estate-agent wording: *"a saintly man, all mod cons."*
- **411 «Профессьон де фуа!»** A pretentious French phrase used wrongly. Keep it mispronounced and garbled, e.g. *"Profession de foy!"*. Don't correct it.
- **428 «Ты пошто боярыню обидел, смерд?!»** Quoted as mock-archaic outrage. *"Why hast thou wronged the boyarina, knave?!"*
- **431–435 «Бондарчук? Никулин? …Смоктуновский! Кеша!»** Yakin guesses famous actors, then name-drops a nickname to show he knows Smoktunovsky personally. "Kesha" is short for Innokenty, which English viewers won't know. Use *"Smoktunovsky! Innokenty, old boy!"* or keep "Kesha" with a gloss. The name-dropping is the joke, more than the specific names.
- **454–461 «Ты чьих будешь?… Эта роль ругательная»** Bunsha answers a feudal insult with a Soviet formal complaint. Make each side clearly belong to its register: *"Whose serf art thou?" / "That designation is abusive. I request it not be applied to me."*
- **462–463, 783 «Дом высокой культуры быта»** A parody of Soviet "socialist competition" titles. Make it a pompous official label, such as *"Model Building of Exemplary Domestic Culture"*, and repeat it exactly at 783.
- **477–481 «Аз есмь. Житие моё… Паки, паки… иже херувимы»** Yakin strings together random church words to sound medieval. Use random King James words: *"Verily… mine life… thee, thou… cherubim…"* The tsar's reply should be coarse archaic: *"What life of thine, thou stinking cur?"*
- **485 «Красотою лепа… бровьми союзна»** Old-style praise, including joined eyebrows as a mark of beauty, which sounds strange today. Keep the strangeness: *"fair of face, crimson of lip, brows conjoined."*
- **495 «Шубу с царского плеча»** A real idiom for a gift from someone powerful. *"I grant thee a fur coat off the royal shoulder."*
- **517 / 583 «Красота-то какая! Лепота!»** People use it jokingly for any pleasant view. Pick one archaism (*"Such beauty! Comeliness!"*) and repeat it word for word. The repetition creates the callback.
- **523–524 «Сдвинь брови!»** A stage direction from Miloslavsky to Bunsha, who is playing tsar. *"Frown!"* or *"Knit your brows!"*
- **533 «Гитлер капут!»** This is Bunsha's only "foreign language," taken from war films. Leave it in German.
- **535, 550 «Кемска волост!»** A Swedish accent clips the word, and the con man gives away land without thinking. The territory really was disputed with Sweden. Keep the accent as *"Kemska volost!"*. The meme is the casual giveaway, so keep 541–543 breezy: *"Let them have it! The state won't go broke."*
- **549 «Передай твой король мой пламенный привет»** Bunsha copies the ambassador's broken grammar and adds a Soviet greeting formula. *"Give your king my fiery greeting."* Keep the grammar broken.
- **551–552 «С кондачка не решаются… посоветоваться с товарищами… зайдите на недельке»** Textbook bureaucratic stalling, still quoted today. *"Such matters aren't decided off the cuff. I must consult my comrades. Drop by next week."* Keep "comrades," because it clashes with the medieval setting.
- **555–556 «Ауфвидерзеен! Гудбай! Оревуар! Короче, чао!»** Keep the foreign words as they are. "In short, ciao!" carries the joke.
- **577–578 «За чей счёт этот банкет?»** Who's paying for the feast? Use "banquet" here so it links to line 712.
- **579–581 «Икра… заморская, баклажанная!»** After black and red caviar comes "overseas" caviar, which is cheap Soviet aubergine spread. Use *"eggplant caviar"* or *"aubergine caviar"*, both of which exist in English, so the word "caviar" stays. "Dip" breaks the list.
- **582 «Ну, царь, вздрогнули!»** «Вздрогнули» is a slang toast meaning roughly "brace yourself." *"Down the hatch, Tsar!"* or *"Brace yourself, Tsar!"*
- **587–588, 780–783 «Нажито непосильным трудом… куртка замшевая — три»** Shpak lists stolen goods like a police inventory, with the noun first and the number after. The list keeps growing, which shows he is padding his claim. Keep the inventory format (*"Jacket, suede: three"*) and the exact numbers and order. *"Everything I earned by back-breaking toil"* must stay fixed every time.
- **597 «Оставь меня, старушка, я в печали»** Fake-poetic melancholy. *"Leave me, old woman, I am in sorrow."*
- **614 / 788 «От смерда слышу» → «От самозванца слышу»** This uses the schoolyard comeback formula. Use a structure that can be reused: *"Peasant!" "Peasant yourself!"* then later *"Impostor yourself!"* "Takes one to know one" breaks the parallel unless it is reused both times.
- **645–646 «Очень приятно, царь»** Bunsha introduces himself as "tsar" like a job title, flustered and repeating himself. *"Pleased to meet you. Tsar."* Keep it exactly the same each time.
- **659–667 «Царям за вредность молоко… нервные клетки не восстанавливаются»** He asks for the Soviet hazardous-job perk for tsars. Use *"Tsars ought to get hazard pay. Free milk!"* For the second line, *"Nerve cells don't grow back"* works as pseudo-medical folk wisdom.
- **668 «Аки пчела»** *"As the bee."* The archaic form is the point.
- **686 «Это нам не тили-тили…»** Bunsha garbles a children's rhyme («Тили-тили, трали-вали, это мы не проходили…»), meaning "we never learned this." English needs nonsense rhyme with an excuse in it: *"Hey diddle-diddle, wasn't in the syllabus."*
- **708 «Танцуют все!»** An official announcement inviting everyone to dance. *"Everybody dance!"*
- **712 «Я требую продолжения банкета!»** Probably the most quoted line in the film. It is formal and indignant. *"I demand the banquet continue!"* Keep "demand" and keep "banquet."
- **722–728 «Аз есмь царь. — Кличка?… Тарапунька и Штепсель»** A police interview treats "tsar" as a criminal alias, and the officer mocks them as a comedy double act. *"Is that your street name?"* … *"Who're you two, Laurel and Hardy?"*
- **732 «Тамбовский волк тебе боярин!»** A twist on a criminal-slang put-down. *"Boyar, my foot!"* or *"Your boyar's in the Tambov woods!"*
- **735 «Казань брал, Астрахань брал, Ревель брал, Шпака не брал»** «Брать» means both "to conquer" and "to rob." "Took Shpak" doesn't mean burglary in English. **Plunder** fits both cities and flats: *"Plundered Kazan, plundered Astrakhan, plundered Reval. Never plundered Shpak."* (Ivan's Reval siege actually failed, so the boast is also a lie.)
- **795–797 «И тебя вылечат… и меня вылечат»** Cheerful acceptance of being taken to a mental hospital. *"They'll cure you too… and me."*
- **800 «Сесть я всегда успею»** «Сесть» means both "sit down" and "go to prison." Set it up with *"sit down to work"* and pay it off with *"I'll get sent down soon enough."* "Sent down" means jailed in British English.
- **813 «Дело шьют!»** Criminal slang, funny coming from the tsar. *"They're trying to frame me!"*

## Other notes
- **«Положь / ложи трубку» → «Положи»** The register gap between uneducated and correct verb forms can only show through tone: *"Put that phone down!"* versus a prim correction.
- **«Ванька… а я, наоборот, Жорж»** Жорж is the French form of Георгий, which is the same name as Юрий. So "Georges Miloslavsky" is a Frenchified version of Zagoskin's hero Yuri Miloslavsky, the novel Khlestakov claims to have written. The literary joke can't be carried over. Do keep the contrast between "Vanka" (common) and "Georges" (fancy French).

## Loose ends
- Speakers for some lines (36, 147, 597, 800) aren't confirmed. Check them against the subtitle file before finalising.
- The source of 686: the research findings attribute it to a Mikhalkov song. I associate the rhyme with the cartoon *Вовка в тридевятом царстве*, but I haven't verified that.
- The claim about the Reval siege comes from memory (the Russian sieges of 1570–71 and 1577 failed). Verify it before putting it in the paper.
- The 1552 Kazan date doesn't need to be explained to viewers. "Two trips" carries the joke.

Evaluation of Culturally Loaded Subtitle Translation: Comparing COMET, LLM-as-Judge, and Meme-MQM
===================================================================================================

BACKGROUND AND HYPOTHESIS
--------------------------
We evaluate subtitle translations of five Soviet comedy films from Russian into English
(and Russian into Galician for one film), produced by GPT-5.2 under eleven translation
prompt conditions. The conditions vary in how much domain knowledge is provided to the
translator: from a bare zero-shot prompt to prompts enriched with character descriptions,
narrative summaries, curated meme lists with analysis, and — in the "given translations"
condition — expert-approved reference translations of the culturally significant passages.

Our central hypothesis is that these translations are broadly fluent and accurate, but
differ specifically in how well they handle culturally loaded content: Soviet-era memes,
idiomatic phrases, wordplay, and allusions that have become part of the cultural fabric
and that require domain knowledge to render appropriately in the target language. We
further hypothesize that standard automatic metrics are ill-suited to detecting this
specific failure mode, because they evaluate the translation as an undifferentiated whole,
diluting or losing the signal from the small number of passages that constitute the actual
test of cultural translation quality.

The "given translations" condition provides expert reference translations as prompts only
for the handful of culturally significant passages (8-23 items per film, depending on the
film). The LLM still translates the full film freely; "given" is not more literal overall,
it simply has better guidance precisely where it is hardest. COMET-kiwi is reference-free
and has no knowledge of any reference translation.

To investigate the hypothesis, we compare three evaluation approaches.


PROCEDURE
---------

1. COMET (reference-free quality estimation)

We evaluate all translation outputs using the wmt22-cometkiwi-da quality estimation model,
a reference-free metric that scores translations based on the relationship between source
and target text without access to any reference translation. Subtitle timecodes are stripped
and the text is sentence-tokenized using Stanza before scoring. We run three translation
samples per method and average the resulting sentence-level COMET scores into a single
system-level score per method.

2. LLM whole-text MQM (holistic LLM-as-judge)

We present the full subtitle text — source and translation, with timecodes removed — to
GPT-5.4 and ask it to identify translation quality issues using a reference-free MQM-style
prompt. The prompt instructs the model to flag major issues (problems that significantly
harm meaning, humor, character voice, or artistic effect, including cultural references
that fail to land) and minor issues (weaknesses in naturalness or style), across four
categories: accuracy, fluency, style, and terminology. The prompt explicitly instructs
the model not to penalize creative solutions that depart from literal meaning but preserve
artistic effect. For texts exceeding the model's context window, the subtitle file is split
into chunks at subtitle entry boundaries. Scores are expressed as major-equivalent errors
per subtitle entry: (major_count x 5 + minor_count) / n_entries.

3. Meme-MQM (culturally grounded item-level evaluation)

Our proposed evaluation targets a curated set of culturally significant passages per film:
8-23 items per film, consisting of phrases, jokes, and allusions that have achieved
near-meme status in Russian popular culture and that represent the core translation
challenge for each film. For each item, a human expert has provided an approved reference
translation and an analysis explaining what makes the passage challenging. GPT-5-mini
(GPT-5.4-mini for Galician) evaluates each candidate translation of each item against
the source text and reference analysis, producing MQM-style issue annotations. Scores
are aggregated across multiple translation and evaluation runs to produce a stable estimate
per method.

4. Meta-evaluation of LLM-as-judge

To understand the nature of LLM-as-judge errors on the culturally significant passages,
we identify which LLM whole-text complaints overlap (via source-quote word overlap) with
the curated reference items, and pass each matched item-method pair to GPT-5.4-mini for
meta-evaluation. For each flagged case, the meta-evaluator receives the Russian source
text, the approved reference translation, the expert analysis, the candidate translation,
and the LLM judge's complaint, and assesses: (1) whether the complaint aligns with what
the expert analysis identifies as the real translation challenge (alignment); and (2)
whether the complaint is unnecessarily harsh given the quality of the translation relative
to the reference (harshness).


FINDINGS
--------

COMET provides no meaningful signal for this task (Table A, Table C). Scores across all
methods fall within a range of 0.003-0.016 across all five films, well within evaluation
noise. The pooled Spearman rank correlation between COMET and Meme-MQM across all
methods and films is rho = -0.02 (p = 0.896), indistinguishable from zero. The metric
has no mechanism for focusing on culturally loaded passages and evaluates the full subtitle
text indiscriminately, making it unable to detect whether the specific passages that
constitute the translation challenge were handled well or poorly.

The LLM whole-text judge ranks the "given translations" method as worst or near-worst on
three of the five films — Ivan Vasilievich, Diamond Arm, and Pokrovskie Vorota — the
Soviet comedies with the densest cultural humor. The pooled correlation between LLM-WF
and Meme-MQM is rho = 0.01 (p = 0.945), also indistinguishable from zero. Notably,
COMET and LLM-WF do correlate negatively with each other (rho = -0.57, p < 0.001),
suggesting they capture some shared surface-level signal that is orthogonal to cultural
translation quality as measured by Meme-MQM.

A coverage analysis reveals that only 14% of all LLM whole-text complaints across
1,928 total issues across five films overlap with a culturally significant passage (Table D).
The judge fires overwhelmingly on ordinary dialogue. Recall of cultural items is higher
(73-91% per film), meaning the judge does eventually mention most items, but this is
driven partly by the volume of complaints rather than targeted attention.

Meta-evaluation of the matched complaints against the expert reference analyses finds
that 81% of the 209 flagged item-method pairs across all five films were rated as
unnecessarily harsh, and only 11% were rated as well-aligned with the actual translation
challenge identified by the expert analysis (Table D). Three systematic failure modes
account for the bulk of the errors:

  1. The judge penalizes translations that are identical or near-identical to the approved
     reference translation.
  2. It misidentifies the comedic or cultural dimension being tested, targeting the wrong
     aspect of the passage.
  3. It raises complaints about entirely different lines in the film whose source quote
     happened to overlap with the cultural item via fuzzy matching.

Meme-MQM ranks the "given translations" method first on all five films, by a substantial
margin (Table B). On Ivan Vasilievich the score for "given" is 0.053 major-equivalent
errors per entry, compared to 0.320 for the next-best method. This is the expected result:
"given" is the only method that receives expert-approved reference translations precisely
for the passages being evaluated, and the evaluation is designed to measure performance
on exactly those passages.


CONCLUSION
----------
Both COMET and LLM-as-judge evaluate the translation as an undifferentiated whole, which
makes them structurally incapable of detecting quality differences that are localized to
a small number of culturally significant passages within an otherwise fluent translation.
The near-zero pooled correlations with Meme-MQM (rho = -0.02 and 0.01 respectively)
confirm that neither metric tracks the same quality dimension. COMET is additionally
insensitive to cultural adaptation by design. The LLM whole-text judge is capable in
principle of noticing cultural failures, but in practice it directs only 14% of its
complaints at culturally significant passages, is unnecessarily harsh in 81% of those
cases, and correctly identifies the actual translation challenge in only 11%. Only an
evaluation grounded in domain-specific knowledge of the culturally significant passages
produces rankings that reflect genuine translation quality on the material that matters.
